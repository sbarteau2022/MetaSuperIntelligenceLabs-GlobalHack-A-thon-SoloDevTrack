## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The falsifiable success criterion

**Teaching text.** "Make it good," "improve support response times," "the model should understand user intent" — these are not success criteria, they are wishes with a deadline. A success criterion is falsifiable when a stranger, handed only the spec and the finished system, could run one specific measurement and produce a single verdict: met or not met. That requires four things stated in advance, before the system exists: (1) a **metric** with an exact computation, not a vibe ("macro-F1 over five ticket categories," not "accuracy"); (2) a **baseline** — what the dumbest reasonable thing already gets you, because a target that doesn't beat a trivial baseline isn't a target, it's a tautology; (3) a **target value** set far enough above baseline to mean something; and (4) for any system with an unevenly-costed failure mode, a **per-slice floor** — a minimum score on the one class or cohort where being wrong is disproportionately expensive, because a single blended number can hide a catastrophic failure inside a flattering average.

That fourth element is the one almost every first-draft spec skips, and it is the one that matters most. A system can look excellent in aggregate and be unshippable on the slice that counts.

**Worked example.** TicketTriage's naive baseline: route every ticket to whatever category is most common in your own back-catalog ("route everything to `bug`, since that's the biggest bucket"). The method: count each label's share of the dataset, take the most common one, and score a classifier that always predicts it — by plain accuracy, and separately by macro-F1 (the unweighted mean of per-class F1, which does not let one large class flatter the average, and which a majority classifier scores badly on almost by construction, since it gets zero recall on every class but its favorite). Lab M01-A has you compute both numbers yourself against the real 31-ticket exemplar set — on paper first, then in code — and its own Verify section is where the actual measured values live, as the answer key to check your prediction against.

A spec that says "beat this baseline" without a number is not falsifiable. TicketTriage's actual success criterion, as written into the spec:

> On a held-out labeled set of ≥150 real tickets, the classifier achieves **macro-F1 ≥ 0.75** overall, **and** **recall ≥ 0.95 on `urgent_escalation` specifically**, measured after any prediction with confidence < 0.6 is routed to a human instead of auto-routed.

Both halves are load-bearing. The macro-F1 target (0.75) is a large, meaningful jump over the majority-class baseline computed in Lab M01-A — not a rounding error. The `urgent_escalation` recall floor (0.95) exists because the cost of a miss is not symmetric: missing a `how_to` ticket costs a slightly annoyed customer; missing an `urgent_escalation` ticket — "our production data is being deleted" — costs a company its customer. A single macro-F1 number cannot see that asymmetry. The floor is what makes it visible.

**Named misconceptions.**
- *"A high aggregate score means the system is good."* An aggregate metric is a weighted average across classes you may not weight the way the business does. Lab M01-C makes this concrete: it is entirely possible for a classifier to clear its aggregate macro-F1 bar comfortably while missing the recall floor on the one safety-critical class — the aggregate says ship it, the floor says don't, and only the floor is right. Predict which way it goes before you run the lab; the real numbers are in its Verify section.
- *"The success criterion should describe what the system does."* No — a success criterion describes **how you will know**, after the fact, whether it worked. "Classifies support tickets into five categories" is a description. "macro-F1 ≥ 0.75 on a 150-ticket held-out set" is a criterion. If you can't compute a pass/fail from the sentence, it isn't one.
- *"Setting the target too high is the safe choice."* An unreachable target doesn't produce a better system — it produces a spec nobody can honestly certify as done, so the certification quietly becomes political ("close enough," "the demo looked fine") instead of measured. A target with no teeth is worse than no target, because it looks like rigor while functioning as theater.

### Lesson 2 — Scope cuts: MUST, SHOULD, WON'T, and the justification each one owes

**Teaching text.** Every real project has more good ideas than build hours. A scope cut is the decision about which good ideas ship in v1, which wait, and which are refused — made once, in writing, before the pressure of a deadline can make it silently for you. The discipline is not "cut ruthlessly"; it's **cut legibly**: every feature that doesn't make v1 gets a one-line reason a stranger could evaluate, not a shrug. "WON'T: multi-language support — no evidence any current customer needs it, and it does not move the success criterion" is a scope cut. Silence is not.

A mechanical scoring rule (impact against the success criterion, divided by estimated cost, greedily filled against a fixed budget) is a useful *first pass* — it removes "whoever argues loudest wins" from the process. But a mechanical rule is not a substitute for judgment, and the sharpest misconception in this lesson is trusting it as one. A pure cost/impact greedy algorithm optimizes total impact per hour; it has no idea that one particular candidate is the only thing that can *measure* whether the success criterion was met. It will cut your own eval set if the budget is tight enough, and it will be "correct" by its own arithmetic while producing a spec that cannot certify itself. The fix is not a smarter algorithm — it's a human sanity pass that pins anything required to measure the success criterion into MUST regardless of what the greedy score says, and says so explicitly.

**Worked example.** Consider a smaller illustration of the same pitfall, using different numbers from TicketTriage's own scope cut so the mechanism is visible before the lab asks you to reason about the real ones. Five candidates, an 8-hour budget, scored by `impact / est_hours`:

```
A: 2h, impact 5 (score 2.50)
B: 2h, impact 4 (score 2.00)
C: 3h, impact 5 (score 1.67)
D: 6h, impact 5 (score 0.83) — required_for_measurement
E: 5h, impact 2 (score 0.40)

Greedy fill, ranked by score: A (2h, running 2h) -> B (2h, running 4h)
-> C (3h, running 7h) -> D needs 6h but only 1h of budget remains, so D
drops to SHOULD -> E never even reaches consideration.
```

Read that trace again: D is not a weak candidate — its impact score ties for the highest in the whole set. It loses purely because its hours no longer fit what three smaller, earlier-ranked items already spent. Nothing was broken in the arithmetic; the algorithm did exactly what it was told. It was told the wrong thing. This is the lesson: a scope cut is a judgment call that a formula can *assist*, never *own* — and it's exactly why `required_for_measurement` exists as a flag independent of score. Lab M01-B runs this identical override against TicketTriage's real ten-candidate list, with its own labeled eval set standing in for the role D plays above; work the Predict step there before you check what the greedy pass actually does with it.

**Named misconceptions.**
- *"MUST should be everything important."* If MUST is everything important, it isn't a cut, it's a wish list with a different label — and it will blow the budget the same way an uncapped feature list always does. MUST is bounded by the stated budget, full stop; everything else is SHOULD or WON'T by construction.
- *"WON'T means never."* WON'T means "not in this v1, for this stated reason." A WON'T with a real justification ("no current customer need," "cost exceeds impact by 6x") is a decision you can defend at the final panel. A WON'T with no justification is scope creep's evil twin — scope amnesia — and both get caught the same way: someone asks "why isn't X here" and there's no answer on the page.
- *"The greedy algorithm's output is the scope cut."* As shown above, it is a draft. The measurement floor, the safety-critical dependency, the thing without which you cannot even tell if you succeeded — these must be checked by hand against the success criterion from Lesson 1, not left to a cost/impact ratio that has never heard of the success criterion.

### Lesson 3 — The riskiest assumption, and the cheap test that retires it before the expensive build

**Teaching text.** Every spec rests on at least one belief that, if false, invalidates the whole plan — not a risk you're willing to accept, but the one thing you are *betting the project on*. Name it wrong and you build for weeks toward a wall you could have found on day one. The discipline has three parts: (1) **state the assumption as a falsifiable claim** ("a single zero-shot LLM prompt, no fine-tuning, no per-customer taxonomy, reaches macro-F1 ≥ 0.75 with `urgent_escalation` recall ≥ 0.95"), not a hope; (2) **pre-register the pass/fail threshold before you run the test** — deciding the bar after seeing the number is not a test, it's a rationalization with a chart; (3) **test it with the cheapest possible harness first**, because the goal is retiring uncertainty per dollar, and a harness that costs nothing to run and can still be wrong in the same direction as the expensive system is worth building before the expensive system is.

That third point is where TicketTriage's exemplar earns its keep. The real system's classifier will eventually be a hosted-LLM API call. But the *harness* — the dataset, the metric, the decision gate — does not require a single API call to test. Build and run the harness against a cheap deterministic stand-in first. If the harness itself is broken (wrong labels, a metric that doesn't compute what you think, a threshold that was never really pre-registered), a real LLM call will not reveal that — it will just hide the bug behind a more expensive source of noise. Prove the test rig works, cheaply, before spending money confirming what the rig should have told you for free.

**Worked example.** The pre-registered gate: `macro_f1 >= 0.55` **and** `urgent_escalation.recall >= 0.90`, decided by checking both conditions and returning `GO` only if both hold. Notice what the gate is defending against in the abstract, before you run anything: on any classifier where one class matters more than the others, it is entirely possible for the aggregate score to clear its bar comfortably while the single safety-critical class misses its own — a strong average can be built out of being good everywhere else while quietly failing the one class you can't afford to fail. A learner who implements the decision gate using only the aggregate score risks a false `GO` — the wrong, gameable answer, and gameable in exactly the direction that matters, because a false `GO` ships silently while a false `NO_GO` only costs you another look. This is the whole reason the assumption gets tested with a gate that checks *both* numbers, not just the one that's easiest to compute: a cheap stand-in run in fifteen minutes can surface a real, specific, addressable gap in an assumption before a single dollar is spent on the real model and before a single week is spent building the rest of the pipeline around it — but only if the gate is actually built to catch the gap instead of averaging past it. Lab M01-C runs this exact gate against the real keyword-rule stand-in and TicketTriage's real dataset, and traces exactly which ticket the stand-in gets wrong and why, in its own Verify section.

**Named misconceptions.**
- *"The riskiest assumption is whatever feels scariest."* It is whatever, if false, invalidates the most already-planned work. "The API might be slow" is a real risk but rarely the riskiest one — you can fix latency without redesigning anything. "The core classification approach doesn't clear the safety floor" invalidates the scope cut, the timeline, and possibly the whole approach. Rank by blast radius, not by anxiety.
- *"A cheap proxy test is a waste of time — just test the real thing."* The real thing costs real money and real weeks. A cheap proxy that shares the same harness (same dataset, same metric, same gate) tests whether the *test itself* is sound, which the expensive version cannot verify about itself. Skipping the cheap version means your first sign of a broken harness is a confusing result from an expensive one.
- *"If the aggregate number clears the bar, the assumption is confirmed."* This is Lesson 1's misconception again, now aimed at the wrong stage. A GO/NO-GO decision has to inherit the same per-slice floor the success criterion has — checking only the number that's easiest to compute, instead of the number the spec actually promised, is how a NO_GO gets rubber-stamped into a GO.

### Lesson 4 — The spec as a judgment artifact: writing so a stranger can grade "done"

**Teaching text.** The test of a spec is not whether the author understands it — the author always understands it, that's the illusion every bad spec runs on. The test is whether someone who has never spoken to the author can read the spec, then look at the finished system, and reach the same verdict the author would reach, using only what's on the page. That's the same judgment-trail standard this curriculum has applied to every prediction, every eval report, every incident writeup since AIE-100: the artifact has to carry the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

Concretely, that means every section of the spec answers a question a hostile stranger would actually ask: *What exactly are you measuring, and against what number?* (Lesson 1's falsifiable criterion.) *What did you decide not to build, and why?* (Lesson 2's scope cut, with justifications, not a bare list.) *What's the one thing that could sink this, and how — cheaply — will you know before it's too late to change course?* (Lesson 3's riskiest assumption, pre-registered, tested.) And the question this course's ethics thread adds on top of the last three, which a spec can satisfy technically while still failing morally: *who bears the cost if this system is wrong, and did they get any say in that number?* A `urgent_escalation` recall floor of 0.95 was, implicitly, a decision about how much of a customer's outage risk this project is willing to accept on their behalf. Writing that floor down is not optional paperwork — it is the only place in the entire project where that tradeoff becomes visible enough to be questioned.

**Worked example.** Compare two one-line entries from a hypothetical TicketTriage spec:

> *Weak:* "We'll try to catch urgent tickets quickly."
> *Stranger-checkable:* "urgent_escalation recall ≥ 0.95 on a 150-ticket held-out set, verified before auto-routing is enabled; a miss routes a real customer outage to the `how_to` queue with next business-day response — the cost is borne by whichever customer's ticket gets misrouted, and that floor was set after asking the support lead what recall rate she could actually live with, not derived from a model card."

The second version is checkable, names the failure mode in concrete terms, and names who pays for a miss and how the number was actually chosen — not asserted from nowhere.

**Named misconceptions.**
- *"A longer spec is a more rigorous spec."* Length hides gaps as often as it closes them. A four-line spec with a real number, a real cut, and a real test beats twelve pages of prose with none of the three.
- *"The spec is done when it feels complete to me."* It's done when it passes the stranger test: could someone with zero conversations with you determine pass/fail from the document alone? If any grading question ("is X in scope," "did this hit target," "was the assumption tested") requires asking the author, the spec has a gap at that exact point.
- *"Ethics considerations belong in a separate section, if at all."* Tier 2 of this course's ethics thread is precisely this: every capstone is a claim about what should exist, and the defense will ask whether it should. A spec that states a per-class floor without stating who that floor protects and who decided it is technically complete and ethically silent — and the panel in the final defense module is built to notice the silence.

```python
# lab_a_solution.py
from __future__ import annotations
from collections import Counter
from dataclasses import dataclass
from dataset import TICKETS, LABELS

def majority_baseline(train_labels: list[str], n: int) -> list[str]:
    counts = Counter(train_labels)
    top_label, _ = counts.most_common(1)[0]
    return [top_label] * n

def compute_metrics(y_true: list[str], y_pred: list[str], labels: list[str]) -> dict:
    per_label = {}
    for label in labels:
        tp = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if t == label and p == label)
        fp = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if t != label and p == label)
        fn = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if t == label and p != label)
        precision = tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) else 0.0
        recall = tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) else 0.0
        f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall) if (precision + recall) else 0.0
        per_label[label] = {"precision": precision, "recall": recall, "f1": f1, "support": tp + fn}
    macro_f1 = sum(v["f1"] for v in per_label.values()) / len(labels)
    accuracy = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if t == p) / len(y_true)
    return {"per_label": per_label, "macro_f1": macro_f1, "accuracy": accuracy}

@dataclass
class SuccessCriterion:
    metric_name: str
    baseline_value: float
    target_value: float
    per_class_floor_label: str
    per_class_floor_metric: str
    per_class_floor_value: float

    def is_falsifiable(self) -> bool:
        return (
            self.target_value > self.baseline_value
            and self.per_class_floor_label in LABELS
            and 0.0 < self.per_class_floor_value <= 1.0
        )

TICKETTRIAGE_SUCCESS = SuccessCriterion(
    metric_name="macro_f1",
    baseline_value=0.0737,
    target_value=0.75,
    per_class_floor_label="urgent_escalation",
    per_class_floor_metric="recall",
    per_class_floor_value=0.95,
)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    y_true = [l for _, l in TICKETS]
    y_base = majority_baseline(y_true, len(y_true))
    metrics = compute_metrics(y_true, y_base, LABELS)
    print("baseline accuracy:", round(metrics["accuracy"], 4))   # 0.2258
    print("baseline macro_f1:", round(metrics["macro_f1"], 4))   # 0.0737
```

```python
# lab_b_solution.py
from __future__ import annotations
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class CandidateFeature:
    name: str
    est_hours: float
    impact: int
    required_for_measurement: bool = False
    justification: str = ""

    @property
    def score(self) -> float:
        return self.impact / self.est_hours

CANDIDATES = [
    CandidateFeature("keyword-rule fallback classifier",         3,  5),
    CandidateFeature("zero-shot LLM classifier call",             4,  5),
    CandidateFeature("confidence threshold + human-review queue", 3,  5),
    CandidateFeature("urgent_escalation Slack alert",             2,  4),
    CandidateFeature("labeled eval set (150 real tickets)",       5,  5, required_for_measurement=True),
    CandidateFeature("per-class metrics dashboard",               3,  3),
    CandidateFeature("admin UI to relabel misroutes",             8,  2),
    CandidateFeature("multi-language ticket support",             10, 2),
    CandidateFeature("auto-suggested canned replies",             6,  2),
    CandidateFeature("fine-tuned in-house classifier model",      20, 3),
]
BUDGET_HOURS = 14.0

@dataclass
class ScopeCutResult:
    must: list = field(default_factory=list)
    should: list = field(default_factory=list)
    wont: list = field(default_factory=list)
    override_used: bool = False

def scope_cut(candidates: list[CandidateFeature], budget_hours: float) -> ScopeCutResult:
    ranked = sorted(candidates, key=lambda c: c.score, reverse=True)
    result = ScopeCutResult()
    spent = 0.0
    for c in ranked:
        if spent + c.est_hours <= budget_hours:
            result.must.append(c)
            spent += c.est_hours
        elif c.impact >= 4:
            c.justification = c.justification or (
                f"high impact ({c.impact}) but exceeds remaining budget "
                f"({budget_hours - spent:.1f}h left, needs {c.est_hours}h) — v1.1 candidate"
            )
            result.should.append(c)
        else:
            c.justification = c.justification or (
                f"low impact ({c.impact}) relative to cost ({c.est_hours}h) — "
                f"does not move the success criterion, cut for v1"
            )
            result.wont.append(c)
    return result

def enforce_measurement_floor(result: ScopeCutResult) -> ScopeCutResult:
    for bucket in (result.should, result.wont):
        for c in list(bucket):
            if c.required_for_measurement and c not in result.must:
                bucket.remove(c)
                c.justification = "pinned: required to measure the success criterion, budget overridden"
                result.must.append(c)
                result.override_used = True
    return result
```

```python
# lab_c_solution.py
from __future__ import annotations
from dataclasses import dataclass
from lab_a_solution import TICKETS, LABELS, compute_metrics

KEYWORD_RULES = [
    ("urgent_escalation", ["urgent", "immediately", "critical", "outage", "security issue", "locked out", "down for"]),
    ("billing", ["invoice", "charged", "refund", "billed", "coupon", "subscription", "plan", "$"]),
    ("bug", ["error", "crash", "crashes", "bug", "blank screen", "zero results", "500", "doesn't", "didn't", "stopped", "resets"]),
    ("feature_request", ["please add", "would be great", "requesting", "any plans", "we'd love", "can you add"]),
    ("how_to", ["how do i", "how can i", "where do i find", "what's the difference", "is there a guide", "what formats"]),
]

def keyword_classify(text: str) -> str:
    t = text.lower()
    for label, keywords in KEYWORD_RULES:
        for kw in keywords:
            if kw in t:
                return label
    return "how_to"

@dataclass
class GateThresholds:
    macro_f1_min: float
    floor_label: str
    floor_metric: str
    floor_min: float

TICKETTRIAGE_GATE = GateThresholds(
    macro_f1_min=0.55, floor_label="urgent_escalation", floor_metric="recall", floor_min=0.90,
)

def decide(metrics: dict, gate: GateThresholds) -> str:
    macro_ok = metrics["macro_f1"] >= gate.macro_f1_min
    floor_value = metrics["per_label"][gate.floor_label][gate.floor_metric]
    floor_ok = floor_value >= gate.floor_min
    return "GO" if (macro_ok and floor_ok) else "NO_GO"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    texts = [t for t, _ in TICKETS]
    y_true = [l for _, l in TICKETS]
    y_pred = [keyword_classify(t) for t in texts]
    metrics = compute_metrics(y_true, y_pred, LABELS)
    print("macro_f1:", round(metrics["macro_f1"], 4))                                   # 0.8449
    print("urgent_escalation recall:", metrics["per_label"]["urgent_escalation"]["recall"])  # 0.8
    print("decision:", decide(metrics, TICKETTRIAGE_GATE))                              # NO_GO
```

## Assessment

The Build step of this module is not another lab — it is the learner's own spec, written against the same template TicketTriage was scored against above, for whatever real problem they've selected as their AIE-401 capstone. It is self-assessed (and later peer- and instructor-reviewed) against the rubric below, which grades exactly the same four things Lessons 1–4 taught, and nothing else. Choice of problem is never graded; only whether a stranger could read the resulting spec and know what "done" means.

**Spec template — every field is required.**

```
1. PROBLEM STATEMENT
   One paragraph: who has this problem, what it costs them today, why now.

2. USERS & STAKEHOLDERS
   Who benefits if this works. Who is exposed to failure, and how.

3. FALSIFIABLE SUCCESS CRITERION
   - metric name + exact computation
   - baseline value + how it was measured (must be a real number, not "TBD")
   - target value (must clear baseline by a stated, meaningful margin)
   - per-slice/per-class floor(s): which failure mode is disproportionately
     costly, the minimum acceptable score on it, and why that number
   - measurement method: what data, how much, how held out

4. SCOPE CUT
   - MUST (bounded by a stated hour/dollar budget — show the arithmetic)
   - SHOULD (each with a one-line justification)
   - WON'T (each with a one-line justification)
   - explicit confirmation that anything required to measure #3 is in MUST,
     even if a mechanical cost/impact score would have placed it elsewhere

5. RISKIEST ASSUMPTION
   - stated as a falsifiable claim, not a hope
   - why it's the riskiest (what breaks if false — blast radius, not anxiety)
   - the cheapest test that could falsify it, and by when it will run
   - the pre-registered pass/fail threshold (stated BEFORE the test runs)

6. COST OF BEING WRONG
   - who bears the cost of each major failure mode
   - whether that person/group had any say in the floor set for it
   - what happens to them, concretely, the day the system is wrong

7. NON-GOALS / OUT OF SCOPE
   - what this system explicitly will not attempt, stated plainly enough
     that a reviewer six months from now can't accuse you of scope creep
     you never disclosed
```

**Gaming-resistant rubric.**

| Dimension | 0 — Fails | 1 — Partial | 2 — Meets bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Falsifiability** | No numeric target, or target/baseline missing, or metric can't be computed from the stated data | Numeric target and baseline present, but no per-slice floor despite an obvious asymmetric failure mode | Numeric metric, real baseline, target with a stated margin over baseline, AND a per-slice floor with a stated reason for that specific slice |
| **Scope cut** | No MUST/SHOULD/WON'T split, or MUST has no budget bound | Split exists and budget is bounded, but WON'T/SHOULD items lack justifications, or nothing checks the measurement floor against MUST | Budget-bounded MUST, every SHOULD/WON'T justified in one checkable sentence, and an explicit statement confirming the success-criterion's measurement requirement survived the cut |
| **Riskiest assumption** | No named assumption, or the "test" has no threshold and no date | Assumption named and falsifiable, but the threshold was set after or alongside the test (not pre-registered), or the test is as expensive as the real build | Assumption stated as a falsifiable claim, threshold pre-registered before any test ran, and the test plan is the cheapest available falsifier — not the full build |
| **Stranger-readability** | A grader would need to ask the author at least one clarifying question to render pass/fail on any of sections 3–5 | Mostly self-contained; one section requires inference beyond what's written | A grader with zero conversations with the author can determine pass/fail on every numbered section from the document alone |
| **Cost of being wrong** | Section 6 is missing or generic ("failures will be minimized") | Names a failure mode and who is affected, but not whether they had a say in the floor | Names who bears the cost of the worst realistic failure, states plainly whether that person/group had input into the floor that was set for them, and does not soften the answer when it's "no" |

A spec cannot pass by being long, confident, or well-written in prose — it passes by being checkable. The grading question for every dimension is always "could a stranger apply this rubric row to the document and reach a number without asking the author anything," which is the same standard Lesson 4 taught and the same standard the labs' pytest suites enforced mechanically: `test_success_criterion_is_falsifiable`, `test_greedy_cut_alone_is_not_trusted_blindly`, and `test_decision_is_no_go_despite_strong_aggregate_score` are all, in code, the same check this rubric applies in prose.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime.

- **Wall — the success criterion arrives as a description, not a criterion.** ("The system will accurately classify tickets.") This is the single most common stall in this module and it looks like progress, because the sentence reads as reasonable. **Reroute:** ask one question — "what specific number, computed how, would make you say no, this failed?" If the learner cannot answer in one sentence, the spec isn't ready to move past Lesson 1 regardless of how much of the rest is drafted.
- **Wall — MUST list absorbs everything.** A learner who is emotionally invested in their own capstone idea will resist cutting anything, and the budget bound gets quietly ignored ("just a rough estimate"). **Reroute:** make the arithmetic non-negotiable before the conversation about *which* features — sum the hours, show the number exceeds budget, and only then discuss what moves. Cutting in the abstract is much harder than cutting against a number already on the page.
- **Wall — riskiest assumption is stated but untestable, or tested too expensively.** ("Our riskiest assumption is that users will like it" — untestable as written; or "we'll know by building the full v1 and shipping it" — not cheap, not early.) **Reroute:** ask "what is the $0, same-day version of this test?" Lab M01-C's keyword-rule stand-in exists exactly to demonstrate that a cheap proxy is almost always constructible even when it doesn't feel like it.
- **Signal — a learner whose own probe returns NO_GO on their real project's riskiest assumption, and who is visibly deflated.** This is exactly on track. Log `struggle-productive`, never `blocked` — a NO_GO discovered in module 1, on a $0 test, is the single best possible outcome of this module; the alternative is discovering it in module 4 after weeks of build. Reassure and move to: does the spec's scope cut or success criterion need to change in response, and is that itself worth a documented revision?
- **Wall — Cost of Being Wrong section is generic or skipped.** Learners will often fill sections 1–5 competently and then write something anodyne for section 6 ("we'll monitor closely and respond to issues"). This is not a content gap, it's an avoidance pattern — naming who specifically eats a specific cost is uncomfortable. **Reroute:** ask for the worst single realistic incident in one sentence, describe the one real person or group it happens to, and ask directly whether that person had any input into the floor. Do not accept "we'll ask users later" as an answer to "did they get a say" — later is not the same as before the floor was set.
- **Accelerate — a learner who reaches a correctly falsifiable success criterion, a defensible scope cut, and a genuinely cheap pre-registered assumption test inside the first working session.** Such a learner can skip re-deriving TicketTriage's exact numbers by hand and move straight to drafting their own spec against the template, using the exemplar only as a reference to check format and rigor against.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to spec-writing and problem selection — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Every large engineering organization that ships reliably has some version of this discipline in writing: a one-page or six-page document with a measurable goal, an explicit non-goals section, and a pre-mortem of what could go wrong, reviewed *before* code is written, not produced afterward to describe what already shipped. Post-incident review culture exists precisely because specs without falsifiable criteria and named risks are where outages are born quietly, months before the pager goes off. This is documented industry practice, not house style.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** The uncomfortable fact "make a great spec" trainings rarely say out loud: specs are very often written *after* the build, to rationalize what was already decided, dressed up as having come first. A success criterion invented to match a result already achieved is not a criterion, it's a certificate of participation with a due date attached. The same pressure that produces that pattern also produces specs whose scope cuts quietly drop safety and measurement infrastructure, not just nice-to-haves — "MVP" becomes the polite word for "we didn't build the part that would have told us we were wrong."
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your spec's per-class floor protects whoever is on the wrong end of your riskiest failure mode. Ask, honestly: whose complaint, whose outage, whose bad day made it into that number — and whose didn't, because you never had the conversation? A spec can be technically flawless by every row of the rubric above and still only encode the costs of the people you happened to talk to. Six modules from now, at the defense, the panel's last question will be some version of this one. It is worth having an honest answer before then, not one assembled under questioning.
