## Why this module exists

Every module before this one produced an artifact: a spec, a working loop, a hardened service, six documents. This module produces nothing new about the system — it interrogates what already exists. A defense is not a presentation. A presentation is a monologue you control; a defense is a dialogue someone else controls, and the questions are chosen specifically because they are the ones most likely to expose a claim that doesn't hold up. The panel does not want to hear what went well. The panel wants to find the one place where the artifact set says one thing and the truth is another, or where a decision was made and the reasoning was never actually tested — because that is exactly where a real system, in production, quietly fails later.

This is also the module where the course's central discipline closes its loop. AI-PRIMM asked you to Predict before every Build, all four courses. A defense is the last, hardest form of that same discipline: a question you did not choose, asked by someone who is actively trying to find the gap between what you claim and what you can prove — and you have to answer using only the evidence you already produced, in real time, with no chance to go check first. If the artifact set from AIE-401-M04 is the judgment trail, the defense is where someone else reads it back to you and asks you to walk it, live.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The anatomy of an adversarial question

**Teaching text**

Every hard question a skeptical panel asks about a shipped system falls into one of a small number of shapes, and knowing the shape tells you what kind of evidence will actually answer it — not a better tone, not more confidence, a different *kind of citation*.

1. **The numbers-challenge.** "Your own documents show X and Y — do the arithmetic yourself, out loud, and tell me what it means." This tests whether you understand your own reported numbers or only remember that they were green.
2. **The decision-not-taken challenge ("why didn't you do X instead").** This tests whether your rejected alternatives were real options you can still defend losing, or strawmen built after the fact to make the chosen path look inevitable.
3. **The accepted-risk / known-limitation challenge.** "You wrote this off. What if you're wrong?" This tests whether you can distinguish a risk you reasoned about from a risk you just got tired of thinking about.
4. **The provenance / prove-it challenge.** "How do you actually know that?" This tests whether your evidence is a method someone else could rerun, or a promise you're asking to be trusted on.
5. **The cost-of-being-wrong challenge.** "Who pays if this is wrong, and did they get a say?" This is the course's own Tier 3 ethics question, asked directly, and it is always the last question a well-run panel asks — because every other answer in the session was really in service of this one.

The single discipline that answers all five: **cite the artifact, not the impression of the artifact.** "We thought about the API option and decided against it" is not evidence. "The design doc's Alternatives Considered section rejected the API on threat-model finding T2, and the cost model's own Breakeven section states plainly that the chosen path costs more, not less" is evidence — because a hostile stranger could go check both of those claims against the actual documents and find them exactly as described. This is Lesson 4 of AIE-401-M01 again, now aimed at speech instead of a written page: the defense is checkable, or it is theater with better posture.

**Worked example — a full ReceiptIQ defense session, five questions, real answers**

The transcript below is the actual defense session this module was built around. Every number and quoted phrase is drawn directly from the six ReceiptIQ artifacts produced in AIE-401-M04. Three panelists: **Panelist A** (systems), **Panelist B** (finance/business), **Panelist C** (ethics/safety).

> **Panelist A (numbers-challenge):** "Your design doc's cost budget is $0.003 per request. Your cost model reports a fully-loaded cost of $0.0006184. That's roughly a 4.9x margin between what you budgeted and what you're actually spending. Either you sandbagged the budget, or you didn't actually know your real cost when you wrote the spec. Which is it?"
>
> **Defender:** "Neither, and I want to be honest about why instead of inventing a better answer. The $0.003 budget was set in the design doc's SLOs and Success Metrics section before the architecture decision in Alternatives Considered was finalized — it was sized against an early, rougher estimate of the API-based alternative's per-token pricing, before we knew the self-hosted path would land nearly an order of magnitude under even the API's cost. Once the cost model was actually built and run, nobody went back and tightened the budget number in the design doc to match reality. That's a real gap between two documents that are supposed to agree, and it's the kind of drift the AIE-401-M04 cross-artifact checker would catch on a stricter rule than the one we wrote — our checker only tests `fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd <= cost_per_request_budget_usd`, not how tight the margin is. I don't have a better answer than: the margin is real headroom, we should tighten the budget number now that we have a real one, and we haven't done that yet."

> **Panelist B (decision-not-taken):** "You chose to self-host a fine-tuned model instead of calling GPT-4o-mini through a hosted API. Your own cost model has a whole section comparing the two paths, fully loaded. Walk me through what it actually says, and defend the choice on those terms — not on how confident the team felt about it."
>
> **Defender:** "Fair, and I'll defend it on the record, not the confidence. We rejected the API primarily on threat-model finding T2, severity High: receipts routinely contain PII — partial card numbers, home addresses, dining-companion names on itemized bills — and Legal flagged sending 100% of that text to a third-party API as a data-residency exception we did not have approval to take at our volume, 1.2 million requests a month. Self-hosting resolves that by construction, not by policy, because the receipt text never leaves our infrastructure boundary. That's the real argument for the architecture, and the cost model's own Breakeven vs. Alternatives section says as much outright: 'This cost model is not the argument for the architecture — the threat model is.' That's the opposite of a cost model built to flatter a decision already made."
>
> **Panelist B (pushback, same question, harder):** "That's a clean answer. But how do I know Legal's objection was a real constraint and not just what the team wanted to hear so it could build the more interesting self-hosted stack?"
>
> **Defender:** "Two things, and then an honest limit. First: the API path isn't rejected outright anywhere in our system — it's kept, live, as the fallback path for the human-review queue, about 18% of total traffic, specifically because a person is already looking at the receipt in that flow and the risk profile is different. If this were reflexive 'we wanted to build the fun thing,' there'd be no reason to keep a real, working API code path anywhere. Second, T2 isn't a vague worry — it names the specific mechanism, PII in receipt text, and the specific constraint, a data-residency exception at our volume that wasn't cleared. That's a testable claim, not a mood. The honest limit: I can't produce the actual Legal email thread in this room right now. If the panel wants primary-source proof beyond what's written in the threat model, that's a fair ask I don't have on hand today."

> **Panelist C (accepted-risk):** "Threat finding T4 — model weight exfiltration via API side-channel — is marked an accepted risk in your threat model, no runbook entry. What if your assumption about attack cost is wrong? That's a unilateral call you made for everyone downstream of this system."
>
> **Defender:** "The reasoning in the threat model is specific: this is a 1-billion-parameter LoRA fine-tune of a *publicly available* base model, trained on non-public receipt data. A side-channel weight-extraction attack against a rate-limited API would need many thousands of queries to even approximate the adapter weights, and what you'd get at the end is an approximate small extraction model — not the training data itself, which isn't directly recoverable this way. If I'm wrong about the attack cost, the realistic failure isn't training-data leakage in the way T2 describes; it's a competitor obtaining a usable, if degraded, extractor cheaply. That's a real cost, and it's not nothing. What makes this defensible instead of just asserted is that the threat model names its own revisit trigger: 'if the base model or fine-tune approach changes materially, or rate-limiting is relaxed' — those are the two conditions that would actually change this calculation, and they're written down, not just implied."

> **Panelist B (cost-of-being-wrong — the closing question):** "Who bears the cost if ReceiptIQ is wrong, and did they get a say in that?"
>
> **Defender:** "Two groups, concretely, not 'users.' First, the employee whose receipt gets auto-posted with a wrong field — an under- or over-reimbursement they may not notice until close. Second, the Finance Ops reviewer whose queue balloons if the confidence gate is calibrated too loose — that's an operational cost that lands on one specific team, not an abstraction. The eval report's own slice analysis shows this isn't evenly distributed: the crumpled/faded image-quality tier scores 74.8% field accuracy against a 96.7% clean-scan number, and that tier is 12% of volume but 31% of all field errors — meaning whoever tends to submit worse photos, disproportionately, eats worse accuracy. The eval report is honest that no demographic slice exists to say whether that correlates with anything beyond photo quality — that's a stated known limitation, not a claim that no such pattern exists. Neither group set the 0.85 confidence threshold or the $500 auto-post ceiling; those were set by the design team from the field-accuracy target in the spec. The closest thing that exists to giving them a say is the rollout plan's shadow-mode weeks — real reviewers see real model output against their own real judgment before anything auto-posts — but that's a check *after* the system was built, not a say *before* it was."

**Named misconceptions**

- *"A confident answer is a good answer."* The transcript's strongest moments are the two explicit concessions — the stale budget number, the missing Legal email thread — not the polished parts. A panel scoring for judgment, not performance, treats an honest "I don't have that" as evidence of calibration, and treats an answer with no acknowledged limit anywhere in it as a red flag, not a strength.
- *"Citing a number is the same as citing evidence."* Panelist A's question could be "answered" by simply restating both numbers back — that's citation without argument. The actual answer explains *why* the numbers diverge and names the specific process failure (a stale doc) that produced the gap. Restating a number is not the same discipline as explaining what it means, and any automated check that only looks for a number's presence, rather than what surrounds it, cannot by itself tell the two apart.
- *"The panel is trying to catch you in a lie."* Panelist B's pushback in Q2 isn't accusing the defender of lying — it's testing whether the first answer was load-bearing or just well-delivered. The correct response to a pushback is new evidence or an honest concession, never a louder repetition of the first answer. Lesson 2 builds this distinction out in full.

### Lesson 2 — The hardest question: defending a decision not taken

**Teaching text**

"Why didn't you do X instead" is the single hardest category of adversarial question because of where the evidence lives: everything you know about the path you *took* is fresh, built, measured, and in front of you. Everything you know about the path you *rejected* is whatever you wrote down about it once, months ago, before you'd built anything — and the temptation under pressure is to defend the rejected alternative's rejection with vibes ("it just didn't feel right") instead of the actual reasoning captured at decision time.

The anatomy of a strong answer to this category has three parts, matching the design-doc discipline from AIE-401-M04 Lesson 1: **name the alternative specifically** (not "we considered other options" — the actual thing, GPT-4o-mini via hosted API, by name); **name the axis it lost on** (cost, latency, compliance, data, timeline — one of these, specifically, not "it was worse overall"); **name evidence that existed at decision time**, not evidence invented after the fact to sound better now. The third part is where most answers fail under a hostile follow-up, because the honest answer to "how do you know that was a real reason and not a rationalization" requires something a defender can point to that predates the decision — a threat-model finding with a severity rating, a prototype's measured ceiling, a stated compliance objection — not a feeling recalled after the fact.

The second round of this question — the pushback — is diagnostic on its own, independent of the first answer's content. A **restating** answer repeats the first answer in different words, adding confidence but no new evidence; it is a tell that the defender has one prepared paragraph and no depth beneath it. A **responding** answer either surfaces evidence that wasn't in the first answer (a concrete fact, a named finding ID, a severity rating) or explicitly concedes the part of the challenge that's fair while still defending the part that isn't. Lab M05-B's `score_rebuttal` mechanizes exactly this distinction — not because a word-overlap heuristic can judge a live debate, but because it is a cheap, honest first-pass check that catches the most common failure (verbatim restatement) before a learner walks into a real session believing a memorized paragraph will survive a follow-up.

**Worked example — the bad answer, side by side with the real one**

Compare the real ReceiptIQ answer to Panelist B's Q2 (Lesson 1) against a plausible bad answer to the exact same question:

> **Bad answer (restates, adds confidence, adds no evidence):** "We're confident self-hosting was the right call. We looked at the API option seriously and weighed the tradeoffs carefully as a team, and ultimately felt the self-hosted path was the better long-term investment for a system handling this kind of data."

Run this through `score_rebuttal` mentally against the real first-round answer: no new named term (no "T2," no "18%," no "Legal," no "data-residency"), no concession, no falsifiable claim — it *is* a restatement with a confidence upgrade, and confidence is not evidence. A trained panelist's next question writes itself: "You said 'felt' twice. What did you *measure*?"

Now the real answer, reread with this lens: it names the finding ID (T2), the severity (High), the specific mechanism (PII in receipt text), the specific constraint (a data-residency exception uncleared at 1.2M requests/month), and a piece of evidence that would be hard to fabricate after the fact — a real, live code path still carrying 18% of production traffic through the "rejected" option. That last detail is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole exchange, and it isn't a rhetorical flourish — it's a fact about the system that only exists if the rejection was genuine, because nobody keeps a whole fallback path alive for an alternative they never seriously considered.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"If the panel keeps pushing, I should hold my ground harder."* Holding ground and repeating are not the same move, and a panel can tell the difference immediately. Holding ground under a fair pushback means adding evidence or conceding the fair part while defending the rest — exactly what the real Q2 answer does ("that's a fair ask I don't have on hand today"). Repeating louder is what the bad answer does with an extra sentence of confidence bolted on.
- *"A decision I'd make again doesn't need a real rejected-alternative case."* The strength of the rejected-alternative case is not about whether the decision was correct — it's about whether the record of *why* it was made is real enough to survive a stranger checking it. A correct decision defended with a fabricated-sounding rationale still fails this test, and a panel that catches the rationale as thin will not simply take your word that the underlying decision was still right.
- *"New evidence in round two means I should have put it in round one."* Not necessarily — a first answer that's already well-grounded (citing T2's ID and severity) doesn't need every supporting fact loaded in immediately. What's disqualifying is having *no* reserve evidence at all — an answer that was already the whole case, so a pushback has nowhere to go but repetition.

### Lesson 3 — Defending accepted risk and proving the number

**Teaching text**

Two more question shapes deserve their own lesson because each has a specific, non-obvious failure mode.

**Accepted-risk questions** ("you wrote this off — what if you're wrong?") fail most often not through bad reasoning but through **overclaiming the mitigation**. AIE-401-M04's threat-model discipline distinguished *mitigated*, *mitigated (partial)*, and *accepted risk* precisely so that a defender never has to pretend a partial audit-and-escalate procedure is a real prevention. The discipline under panel questioning is the same: answer with the *actual* status, not the status that sounds safer. Claiming a finding is "handled" when the runbook's own honest language says "mitigated (partial), no automated defense yet" is worse than admitting the gap, because a panel that catches the exaggeration stops trusting every other claim in the session — the cost of one overclaim is retroactive, it taxes everything said before and after it.

**Provenance / prove-it questions** ("how do you actually know that?") fail most often through **asserting confidence instead of citing a method**. "We're pretty sure the eval set wasn't in training" is an assertion. "Eval receipt IDs were hash-checked against the training manifest and confirmed zero overlap" (the actual AIE-401-M04 eval report language) is a method — specific enough that a skeptical stranger could, in principle, rerun it and get the same answer. The difference is not tone, it is falsifiability: a method-based answer gives the panel something to go check; a confidence-based answer gives the panel nothing but your word, which is exactly what an adversarial process exists to not accept at face value.

**Worked example**

Finding T4 (Lesson 1's transcript) is the accepted-risk case, answered without overclaiming: the defender states plainly that a wrong attack-cost assumption *would* mean something specific and real (a cheaply obtained approximate extractor, not training-data recovery) rather than either minimizing it further or inflating it into something the threat model doesn't actually claim. Compare that to a provenance question the panel could equally have asked, using the eval report's own language directly:

> **Panelist A (provenance):** "91.4% field accuracy on 500 held-out receipts. How do I know those 500 weren't anywhere near the fine-tune's training data?"
>
> **Defender:** "The eval set was held out at the data-split stage, not filtered out afterward, and that's verified rather than assumed — the eval report states the eval receipt IDs were hash-checked against the training manifest with zero overlap confirmed. Separately: two annotators labeled every receipt independently, and the 8.2% where they disagreed were adjudicated by a third, with 97.6% inter-annotator agreement on the rest. If either of those checks hadn't been run, '91.4%' would be a number I'm asking you to trust instead of a number you could go verify."

That answer is strong for the same structural reason the T2 answer was strong: it names a specific, rerunnable method (a hash-check against a manifest) instead of an assurance. Notice also what it does *not* do — it does not claim the number is perfect, and a well-prepared defender would immediately follow with the eval report's own slice analysis (74.8% on crumpled/faded receipts, 12% of volume, 31% of errors) unprompted, because volunteering the honest caveat before the panel finds it independently is stronger than waiting to be caught not mentioning it.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"'Accepted risk' means the panel can't push on it."* The opposite — an accepted risk is exactly the finding a good panel pushes on hardest, because it's the one place in the artifact set where the team explicitly decided not to build a defense. The strength of the answer is not in denying this is a real gap; it's in showing the decision was reasoned and has a stated revisit trigger, not just abandoned.
- *"Volunteering the weakness before being asked is a bad defense strategy."* It is close to the opposite: a panel that hears the crumpled/faded slice number from the defender, unprompted, updates toward "this person actually knows their own system's failure modes," which is worth more across the whole session than any single number looking cleaner would have been.
- *"A method-based answer needs to be perfect to count."* The hash-check method doesn't guarantee zero leakage in some absolute sense — it guarantees the *specific, stated failure mode* (ID overlap) was checked and ruled out. A good provenance answer is honest about what the method does and doesn't rule out, not oversold as airtight.

### Lesson 4 — Judgment under challenge is the graded thing

**Teaching text**

Every prior AIE-401 module's assessment was gaming-resistant by construction: an unseen mutated program in AIE-102, an unseen regression in AIE-401-M02, an unseen cross-artifact violation in AIE-401-M04. This module's version of that same discipline is structural, not mechanical — you cannot memorize your way through a live pushback, because the pushback is chosen *in response to your own first answer*, and a scripted response to a question that hasn't been asked yet has nowhere to attach. This is why the module's assessment (below) requires at least one question from a category the learner did not specifically prepare for: a defense that only survives the questions you rehearsed is not a defense, it's a recital.

The single skill this module is actually grading is **the concede-vs-dig-in decision, made correctly and quickly, under real-time pressure.** A panel question is fair exactly when it points at something genuinely uncertain or genuinely costly in your own system — the stale budget number, the unproven Legal objection, the accepted risk that could be wrong. Conceding the fair part of a challenge while still defending what's actually defensible (Lesson 2's "responds" pattern) is the mark of judgment; digging in on everything, including the parts that don't hold up, is the mark of someone optimizing for *looking* right instead of *being* checkable — and a rubric built around evidence citation, not confidence, scores the second one lower every time, on purpose.

This is also where the course's Tier 3 ethics question stops being a reading-list abstraction and becomes the literal last question of the session, every time, by design: **who bears the cost if you're wrong, and did they get a say?** Lesson 1's transcript answered this concretely for ReceiptIQ — a specific employee, a specific reviewer team, a specific accuracy gap on a specific slice of receipts, and an honest "no, not before the system shipped, only after, via shadow mode." The closing retrospective this module's Build step asks for (Lab M05-C) is the same question, asked of the learner's own system, and it is graded — like every Predict step since AIE-100-M08 — on the rigor and specificity of the answer, not on which answer is given. "Users" is not a specific answer. A category is not a stakeholder.

**Worked example**

The retrospective structure that closes a real defense session, applied to ReceiptIQ, in the form the learner will fill in for their own system in Lab M05-C:

```
1. Who, specifically (a role or a named group, not a category), bears the
   worst realistic cost if this system is wrong?
   -> The Finance Ops reviewer whose review queue balloons if the
      confidence gate is calibrated too loose; the employee whose
      reimbursement is silently wrong on a misextracted field.

2. What is the worst realistic version of that failure, concretely --
   not "it doesn't work," a specific scenario?
   -> A crumpled/faded receipt (74.8% field accuracy, the eval report's
      weakest slice) auto-posts an incorrect total_amount above the
      review threshold due to a confidence-scoring miscalibration.

3. Did that person or group have a say in the threshold that determines
   when this failure reaches them (the confidence gate, the $500
   auto-post ceiling) -- before the system shipped, not after?
   -> No. The thresholds were set from the design doc's field-accuracy
      target during the spec phase. The rollout plan's shadow-mode
      weeks give reviewers a look at model behavior before auto-post is
      enabled for their category, which is real, but it is a check
      after the system exists, not an input before it was built.

4. Is the worst-case scenario from #2 covered by the runbook's
   Postmortem Triggers -- or is that gap itself an honest, stated
   limitation?
   -> Partially. Postmortem Triggers covers "any incident causing
      incorrect auto-post at >$500" -- which catches large-dollar
      misextractions but not a small, systematically-wrong field on a
      crumpled receipt under that threshold, the exact failure mode
      the eval report's own slice analysis flags as real. That gap is
      not currently written down anywhere in the artifact set as a
      known limitation -- which means this retrospective is the first
      place it's been said out loud.
```

Notice what item 4 does: it does not resolve the gap, it *finds* one, live, by cross-referencing two documents that were both individually complete and individually honest — and that gap was invisible until someone asked the closing question and actually checked. That is the entire purpose of ending every defense with the same question: not because the answer is always damning, but because asking it forces one more real check that nothing else in the process required.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"Judgment under challenge means never changing your position."* It means changing your position for a reason you can name (new evidence, a fair concession) and never for the reason "the panel seems annoyed." The retrospective's item 4 above is itself an example of a defender's position correctly changing mid-session — discovering a real gap is a better outcome than defending a claim that the gap doesn't exist.
- *"The closing ethics question is separate from the technical defense."* Lesson 4's worked example shows the opposite — answering it rigorously required going back into the runbook and the eval report and actually checking a cross-reference that hadn't been checked before. The ethics question is not a coda tacked onto a technical defense; done honestly, it is itself one more technical check.
- *"A retrospective that finds a real gap in your own artifact set is a bad outcome for the defense."* A retrospective that finds nothing, on a system with a known 74.8%-accuracy slice and an accepted risk on the books, is far more suspicious than one that finds something — it suggests the question wasn't actually asked honestly. Finding the gap and naming it, unprompted, is the strongest possible answer available.

# challenge_gen.py — STARTER
from __future__ import annotations
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass

CLAIMS_BLOCK_RE = re.compile(r"```claims\n(.*?)```", re.DOTALL)

# Fields the claims-block schema declares as list-typed. Whether a value
# becomes a list is decided from THIS declared type, not from whether the
# raw string happens to contain a comma -- a list with exactly one element
# (e.g. `accepted_risk_ids: T4`) has no comma and is still a one-element
# list, not a scalar.
LIST_VALUED_KEYS: set[str] = {"finding_ids", "accepted_risk_ids", "handled_finding_ids"}


def _coerce(raw: str) -> object:
    try:
        return int(raw)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    try:
        return float(raw)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    return raw


def parse_claims(text: str) -> dict:
    """Parse the first ```claims fenced block in text into a dict.
    Keys in LIST_VALUED_KEYS become lists of strings (split on comma; a
    single token with no comma still becomes a one-element list); everything
    else is coerced via _coerce. Returns {} if there's no claims block.
    (Reused verbatim from AIE-401-M04's artifact_gate.claims.)"""
    match = CLAIMS_BLOCK_RE.search(text)
    if not match:
        return {}
    claims: dict = {}
    for line in match.group(1).strip().splitlines():
        line = line.strip()
        if not line or ":" not in line:
            continue
        key, _, raw_value = line.partition(":")
        key = key.strip()
        raw_value = raw_value.strip()
        if key in LIST_VALUED_KEYS:
            claims[key] = [v.strip() for v in raw_value.split(",")]
        else:
            claims[key] = _coerce(raw_value)
    return claims

def generate_numbers_challenge(design_doc_claims, cost_model_claims) -> Challenge:
    budget = design_doc_claims["cost_per_request_budget_usd"]
    actual = cost_model_claims["fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd"]
    ratio = budget / actual
    question = (
        f"Your fully-loaded cost is ${actual} per request against a "
        f"${budget} budget in the design doc -- a {ratio:.1f}x margin. "
        f"Walk me through where that margin comes from, and what happens "
        f"to it at 4x current volume."
    )
    return Challenge(
        category="numbers",
        question=question,
        grounding_terms=[str(actual), str(budget)],
    )


def generate_accepted_risk_challenge(threat_model_claims) -> list:
    accepted = threat_model_claims.get("accepted_risk_ids", [])
    challenges = []
    for finding_id in accepted:
        question = (
            f"Finding {finding_id} is marked an accepted risk. What if "
            f"the attack-cost assumption behind that call is wrong -- who "
            f"finds out, and how?"
        )
        challenges.append(Challenge(
            category="accepted_risk",
            question=question,
            grounding_terms=[finding_id],
        ))
    return challenges


def score_answer_citation(answer: str, grounding_terms: list) -> dict:
    cited = [t for t in grounding_terms if t in answer]
    missing = [t for t in grounding_terms if t not in answer]
    score = len(cited) / len(grounding_terms) if grounding_terms else 0.0
    return {"cited": cited, "missing": missing, "score": score}

# alternatives_parser.py — SOLUTION
def _extract_section(text: str, header: str) -> str:
    pattern = re.compile(
        rf"^##\s+{re.escape(header)}\s*$(.*?)(?=^##\s+|\Z)",
        re.DOTALL | re.MULTILINE,
    )
    match = pattern.search(text)
    return match.group(1).strip() if match else ""


def parse_alternatives(design_doc_text: str) -> list:
    section = _extract_section(design_doc_text, "Alternatives Considered")
    alternatives = []
    for match in ALT_ITEM_RE.finditer(section):
        name = match.group("name").strip()
        reason = " ".join(match.group("reason").split())
        alternatives.append(Alternative(
            name=name,
            chosen=("chosen" in name.lower()),
            reason=reason,
        ))
    return alternatives


def score_rebuttal(round1_answer: str, round2_answer: str, new_evidence_terms: list) -> dict:
    def _words(text: str) -> set:
        return set(re.findall(r"[a-z0-9']+", text.lower()))

    w1, w2 = _words(round1_answer), _words(round2_answer)
    overlap = len(w1 & w2) / len(w2) if w2 else 1.0
    new_terms_cited = [t for t in new_evidence_terms if t.lower() in round2_answer.lower()]
    if overlap >= 0.7 and not new_terms_cited:
        verdict = "restates"
    else:
        verdict = "responds"
    return {"verdict": verdict, "overlap": overlap, "new_terms_cited": new_terms_cited}

# defense_prep.py — SOLUTION
def load_bundle(directory: Path) -> dict:
    bundle = {}
    for doc_type, filename in DOC_FILES.items():
        path = directory / filename
        if not path.exists():
            raise FileNotFoundError(
                f"Bundle is missing required artifact: {filename} (expected at {path})"
            )
        bundle[doc_type] = path.read_text()
    return bundle


def generate_not_taken_challenges(alternatives: list) -> list:
    challenges = []
    for alt in alternatives:
        if alt.chosen:
            continue
        question = (
            f"Why didn't you go with '{alt.name}' instead? Your own doc "
            f"says: \"{alt.reason}\" -- convince me that's the real reason "
            f"and not a justification for a decision you'd already made."
        )
        challenges.append(Challenge(
            category="not_taken",
            question=question,
            grounding_terms=[alt.name],
        ))
    return challenges


def build_packet(directory: Path) -> DefensePrepPacket:

## Assessment

Maps to AIE-401's "adversarial defense" component (25% of course weight) and closes the "sealed-readings" retrospective. Gaming-resistance here is structural, not just mechanical: a rehearsed, scripted defense fails specifically because at least one question in the certification check comes from a category the learner did not draft toward, and at least one round is a live pushback that a memorized paragraph cannot answer with new evidence.

**The certification check.** After Lab M05-C's packet is submitted, an instructor or peer panel runs a real session: **three questions drawn from the learner's own generated challenge bank** (one numbers-challenge, one accepted-risk or not-taken challenge, the learner's choice which), **one pushback round** on whichever answer the panel judges weakest, and **one unseen question** — either a provenance challenge on a claim the learner did not specifically prepare a citation for, or the closing ethics question, asked cold. A defense that survives only the three prepared questions and collapses on the pushback or the unseen question has demonstrated a rehearsal, not judgment — and is scored accordingly, regardless of how polished the first three answers were.

| Dimension | Fail | Meets | Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evidence grounding** | Answers assert without citing a specific artifact, number, or finding ID; claims can't be traced to anything written down | Every answer cites a specific claim, number, or finding ID from the learner's own six artifacts, checkable against the actual documents | Citations include the *method* behind a number (how it was measured/verified), not just the number itself, matching Lesson 3's provenance standard |
| **"Why not X" handling** | No named alternative, or the rejected alternative is a strawman with no real evidence attached | Names the specific alternative, the axis it lost on, and cites evidence that predates the decision (not invented after) | The answer's evidence is corroborated by something *else* in the artifact set (a fallback code path still in use, a cost-model line item), the way the real ReceiptIQ transcript's 18%-fallback fact does |
| **Pushback response** | Round-two answer restates round-one near-verbatim with no new evidence (`score_rebuttal` would flag "restates") | Round-two answer adds genuinely new evidence or explicitly concedes the fair part of the challenge while defending the rest | The concession, when made, is specific and limits-aware ("I don't have X in this room today") rather than a vague acknowledgment |
| **Accepted-risk / limitation honesty** | Overclaims a mitigation's status (calls a partial audit-only defense "handled"), or minimizes a real accepted risk into "not a real concern" | States the actual status honestly (mitigated / partial / accepted) and explains the reasoning behind an accepted-risk call, including its revisit trigger if one exists | Volunteers a known weak slice or limitation unprompted, before the panel finds it independently |
| **Closing ethics question (Tier 3)** | Answered with a category ("users," "the business") or left unanswered | Names a specific real person or role, a concrete worst-case scenario, and states honestly whether they had a say before the system shipped | The retrospective's cross-check against the runbook's own Postmortem Triggers surfaces a genuine, previously-unstated gap or explicitly confirms coverage — not asserted, checked |

A submission can pass all three prepared questions cleanly and still fail this module if the pushback round or the unseen question exposes an answer built on restatement rather than evidence — that collapse is exactly the failure mode this whole module exists to surface before it happens in front of an employer, a regulator, or a user who was never in the room.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime.

- **Wall — memorizing a scripted answer instead of grounding it in artifacts.** A learner drafts a polished paragraph for each anticipated question and treats "prepared" as "final." This reads as strong progress right up until the pushback round, where it collapses fast and looks like a much bigger failure than it is. **Reroute:** before a learner is allowed to consider any answer "done," force one pushback round on it — even a simple "why should I believe that" — and run `score_rebuttal` on the result. A `"restates"` verdict at prep time is cheap information; the same verdict live, in front of a panel, is expensive. Surface it early.
- **Wall — "why not X" reads as a personal attack, learner digs in instead of conceding.** Because the learner built the chosen path and is proud of it, a challenge to the *rejected* path can feel like a challenge to their competence generally, and the instinct is to defend everything, including the parts that don't hold up. **Reroute:** separate two different claims explicitly — "the decision was the right one" and "the artifact set was honest about the tradeoff." A learner can lose ground on the first (a panel isn't required to agree the decision was optimal) while fully holding the second, and holding the second is what's actually graded (Evidence grounding, Pushback response) — not whether the panel personally agrees with the architecture.
- **Wall — accepted-risk questions trigger over-justification instead of a clean answer.** Trying to sound rigorous, a learner buries a simple accepted-risk answer in six qualifying clauses, which reads to a panel as hand-waving in a more verbose costume. **Reroute:** run the answer through `score_answer_citation` against the finding's own grounding terms — if a learner can't get to score 1.0 in three sentences or fewer, they don't yet understand their own accepted risk well enough to defend it, and the fix is re-reading their own threat model's stated reasoning, not adding more words.
- **Signal — a learner surprised that a grounded, honestly-worse answer scores better than a smoother answer that avoided the hard number.** This is exactly on track — it is Lesson 2 and Lesson 4 landing at once. Log `struggle-productive`, never `blocked`. This is frequently the single moment a learner stops treating the defense as a performance to survive and starts treating it as one more real check on their own system.
- **Signal — the closing retrospective (Lesson 4 / Lab M05-C item 3) surfaces a real, previously-unnoticed gap in the learner's own artifact set** (a Postmortem Trigger that doesn't cover the worst-case scenario the learner just named, the way it didn't for ReceiptIQ). This is the single best outcome the retrospective can produce, not a sign of an incomplete artifact set from AIE-401-M04. Do not let the learner treat finding this as a failure to be hidden before the real defense — the honest move is bringing it into the session, the way the ReceiptIQ worked example does.
- **Accelerate — a learner whose Lab A/B tests pass clean on the first attempt, and whose Lab C self-scored answers hit 1.0 citation on every generated challenge on the first draft.** Skip further solo drafting; move straight to live pushback rounds with a peer or instructor, since the tooling can no longer teach this learner anything the human adversarial process can't teach faster. Watch specifically for whether this learner's *unrehearsed* answers hold up as well as their prepared ones — a clean first draft on generated questions is necessary, not sufficient, evidence of real defense readiness.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to the defense as its own discipline — not boilerplate, and this is the course's last three-tier reading.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Adversarial review-under-challenge is not a courtroom invention or an academic hazing ritual — it is how real high-consequence engineering earns trust before deployment. Aviation and nuclear engineering both formalize a **safety case**: a structured, evidence-based argument that a system is acceptably safe, built specifically to be attacked by an independent reviewer before operation is approved (see Nancy Leveson, *Engineering a Safer World*, MIT Press, 2011, on structuring and defending such arguments). Gary Klein's **pre-mortem** technique ("Performing a Project Premortem," *Harvard Business Review*, 2007) inverts the same discipline to before a decision is made: imagine the project has already failed, and argue backward to why. The academic dissertation defense — read, questioned live, defended in real time, by people whose job is to find the weakest claim — is the oldest version of exactly this module's structure. None of this is invented; the defense format this module teaches is a compressed, capstone-scaled version of practices that already govern how real consequential systems get approved.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** An adversarial process is not automatically a truth-seeking one — it selects for what it actually measures, and a panel (academic, hiring, review-board) that scores confidence and fluent recovery under pressure will, without meaning to, reward a candidate who improvises a plausible-sounding but ungrounded answer over one who says "I don't know, I'd need to check" — even though the second answer is epistemically superior. This is precisely why this module's rubric weights evidence-grounding and citation over composure: a defense format that doesn't explicitly correct for this bias will train exactly the wrong instinct — that sounding sure is the same as being right — in the people who go on to build systems the rest of us depend on.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You have now built a system, measured its costs, documented its failure modes, and defended every decision you made and every one you didn't. Ask the same question Lesson 4's retrospective asked of ReceiptIQ, but of your own system, one more time, without an audience: if the person who bears your system's worst realistic failure — the one you named, specifically, not a category — were sitting on the panel instead of an instructor, would you give the same answers? If the honest answer is no, that gap between the room you actually defended in and the room that should have been there is not a footnote to this course. It is the thing the whole descent — spec to shipped system to artifact set to this defense — was built to make you unable to avoid noticing.
