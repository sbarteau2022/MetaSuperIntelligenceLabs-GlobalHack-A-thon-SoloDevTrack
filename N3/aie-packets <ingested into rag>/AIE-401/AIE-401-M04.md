## Why this module exists

A demo proves a system runs. It proves nothing about what the system costs, what breaks it, who gets paged when it does, or whether the number on the slide matches the number in the code. The artifact set is what turns "I built something" into "here is the claim I am making about what I built, and here is the evidence." A reviewer — or an adversarial defense panel — does not get a walkthrough. They get the repo, the eval report, the cost model, and the incident runbook, and they get to ask why the numbers in one don't match the numbers in another.

That's the discipline this module teaches: **six specific documents, each with required sections that real industry practice has converged on, cross-checked against each other by code so the mismatches can't hide in prose.**

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The Design Doc and the Model Card: what you're building, and what you built

**Teaching text**

A design doc is a decision record written *before* the system exists in its final form, not a explainer written after. The tell is the **Alternatives Considered** section: if you can't name at least two options you rejected and *why*, the doc was written after the fact to justify what already shipped, and it will not survive a single adversarial question ("did you actually consider X, or are you telling me that now?").

Required sections, drawn from how design docs actually function at companies that run a design-review gate before code review (Google's design-doc culture, Amazon's 6-pagers, Stripe's RFCs all share this skeleton even when the templates differ cosmetically):

1. **Problem Statement** — who hurts, how much, measured, not asserted.
2. **Goals and Non-Goals** — non-goals are load-bearing; they are what you refuse to be blamed for later.
3. **Alternatives Considered** — at least two rejected options and the specific reason each lost.
4. **System Overview** — the architecture as it will actually be built, not an idealized version.
5. **SLOs and Success Metrics** — numbers a machine can check, not adjectives.
6. **Rollout Plan** — how exposure increases and what gates each increase.
7. **Open Questions / Risks** — what you don't know yet, stated plainly.

A model card is a different document with a different job: it is the *nutrition label* for the specific model artifact, not the system around it. The canonical shape (Mitchell et al., "Model Cards for Model Reporting," 2019) is: Model Details, Intended Use, Factors, Metrics, Evaluation Data, Training Data, Ethical Considerations, Caveats and Recommendations. A model card that only has "Model Details" and "Metrics" is a spec sheet, not a model card — it's missing the two sections that exist specifically to stop misuse: **Intended Use** (and by implication, unintended use) and **Ethical Considerations**.

**Worked example — ReceiptIQ Design Doc (full)**

```markdown
# ReceiptIQ — Design Doc

​```claims
p99_latency_slo_ms: 2000
availability_slo_pct: 99.5
cost_per_request_budget_usd: 0.003
field_accuracy_target_pct: 90.0
​```

## Problem Statement
Finance Ops keys ~14,000 expense receipts/month by hand into the accounting
system. Time-motion sampling over 3 weeks (n=210 receipts) measured 6.2 min
average per receipt (data entry + category assignment). A post-hoc audit of
400 randomly sampled entries from the last close found a 4.7% field-error
rate (wrong category, transposed amount, or wrong vendor), each of which
costs an average 11 minutes of accountant rework to catch and fix during
close. At current volume this is ~1,450 accountant-hours/month and a
material source of close-cycle delay.

## Goals and Non-Goals
**Goals**
- Extract structured fields (vendor, date, total_amount, currency, category,
  tax_amount) from OCR'd receipt text with field accuracy that lets the
  majority of receipts auto-post without a human touching them.
- Route anything under-confidence to a human review queue instead of
  guessing.
- Cut accountant hours spent on data entry, not shift the error rate onto
  a machine nobody audits.

**Non-goals (v1)**
- No handwritten receipts. OCR confidence on handwriting is unreliable
  enough that we'd be extracting from garbage; out of scope until OCR
  quality improves.
- No non-English receipts. Fine-tune corpus is English-only; a non-English
  receipt should hit the review queue, not a hallucinated field.
- No receipts with >20 line items (long-tail itemized invoices route to
  review queue; this is a receipt classifier, not a general document
  extractor).

## Alternatives Considered
1. **Rules/regex extraction over OCR text.** Rejected: vendor name and
   category are not regex-shaped problems (vendor strings are inconsistent,
   category requires judgment a regex can't make). Prototyped in AIE-401-M02
   scoping; regex-only ceiling was ~61% field accuracy on the same eval set
   used below.
2. **GPT-4o-mini via hosted API for every request.** Considered seriously —
   it is a capable extractor and would have been the fastest to ship.
   Rejected primarily on the AIE-304-style threat-model finding **T2**
   (below): receipts routinely contain PII (last-4 card digits, home
   addresses on delivery receipts) and Legal flagged sending 100% of receipt
   text to a third-party API as a data-residency exception we did not have
   approval to take at the volume we run (1.2M requests/month). Kept as a
   fallback path for the human-review queue only, where a person is already
   looking at the receipt and volume is ~18% of total traffic.
3. **Fine-tuned small model, self-hosted (chosen).** Reuses the LoRA
   fine-tune from AIE-301 and the vLLM serving path optimized in AIE-303.
   Keeps receipt text inside our infrastructure boundary, which resolves T2
   by construction rather than by policy. Costs more, fully loaded, than
   the API alternative would have (see Cost Model) — that tradeoff is made
   explicitly here, not hidden.

## System Overview
Upstream OCR service (existing, out of scope of this project) emits raw
receipt text. ReceiptIQ API (FastAPI + vLLM continuous batching, 1x-2x L4
GPU) takes OCR text, returns structured JSON + a per-field confidence score.
A confidence gate routes: fields all above threshold AND total_amount under
$500 → auto-post to accounting system via webhook; anything else → human
review queue (existing tool, receives ReceiptIQ's draft extraction
pre-filled for the reviewer to correct, not re-key from scratch).

## SLOs and Success Metrics
- Field accuracy ≥ 90.0% exact-match on held-out eval set (blocks auto-post
  tier if a release regresses below this — see Eval Report).
- p99 latency ≤ 2000ms end-to-end (OCR text in, structured JSON out).
- Availability ≥ 99.5% during business hours (7am–7pm local, when receipts
  are actively submitted from the mobile app).
- Cost per request ≤ $0.003 fully loaded (compute + ops + on-call
  amortized — see Cost Model for what "fully loaded" excludes vs. includes).

## Rollout Plan
Week 1–2: shadow mode — ReceiptIQ runs on all traffic, output logged,
nothing auto-posts, human reviewers do their normal job blind to model
output. Compare model output to reviewer's actual entry.
Week 3: 10% of traffic auto-posts (lowest-risk category: office supplies
under $50), rest stays shadow.
Week 4+: ramp by category as each category's shadow accuracy clears 90%
independently — categories are not released as a block.

## Open Questions / Risks
- Category taxonomy may need to grow (new expense types); retraining
  cadence not yet decided — currently ad hoc, should become quarterly.
- Feedback loop risk: auto-corrected review-queue entries become future
  training data. If reviewers rubber-stamp incorrect model output instead
  of correcting it, the model trains on its own mistakes. Threat model
  finding **T5** tracks this; no automated defense yet, only reviewer
  spot-audits.
```

**Worked example — ReceiptIQ Model Card (full)**

```markdown
# Model Card — ReceiptIQ-extract-v1

## Model Details
- Base model: Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct
- Adaptation: LoRA fine-tune, rank 16, 3 epochs, trained in AIE-301 run
  `receiptiq-lora-run-014`
- Served via vLLM 0.6.x, continuous batching, quantized to FP8 for serving
  (AIE-303 optimization pass; base fine-tune checkpoint is BF16)
- Output format: constrained JSON (vendor, date, total_amount, currency,
  category, tax_amount, per-field confidence)

## Intended Use
Extracting structured expense fields from **English-language, printed or
digitally-generated receipt text that has already passed through OCR**, for
routing to auto-post or human review in the ReceiptIQ pipeline. Intended to
run behind the confidence gate described in the design doc — this model is
not intended to be queried standalone as a general information extractor,
and its output is not intended to auto-post without the gate.

**Out of scope / not intended:** handwritten receipts, non-English receipts,
line-item-level itemization, any use where model output posts to a
financial system without the confidence gate and human-review fallback
described in the design doc.

## Factors
Accuracy varies materially by: image/OCR quality tier (clean scan vs. phone
photo vs. crumpled/faded), vendor category (structured vendors like airlines
extract more reliably than handwritten-style small-vendor receipts), and
receipt length (single-item vs. itemized).

## Metrics
Field-level exact-match accuracy (primary), hallucination rate (field
present in model output with no support in source OCR text), human-review
trigger rate. Full definitions and results in the Eval Report.

## Evaluation Data
500 held-out receipts, stratified by vendor category and image-quality
tier, drawn from the same distribution as training data but excluded from
the LoRA fine-tune split. Hand-labeled by two annotators independently,
adjudicated on disagreement by a third. See Eval Report for full
provenance.

## Training Data
8,400 labeled receipts collected from Finance Ops' existing archive
(historical receipts already keyed by accountants, used as weak labels)
plus 600 hand-corrected examples targeting known weak categories
(restaurant tips, foreign-currency conversions). English-language only.
Skews toward US vendor set (85% of training examples are US vendors) —
this is a known training-data limitation, not just an eval-time one.

## Ethical Considerations
- Receipts frequently contain PII (partial card numbers, home addresses,
  names of dining companions on itemized bills). Training data was not
  scrubbed of this — the model has seen it. See Threat Model finding T2
  for the mitigation (this is a logging/retention control, not a model
  control — the model itself was not adversarially trained to refuse to
  echo PII).
- Auto-post decisions affect what an employee gets reimbursed and how fast.
  A systematic accuracy gap against a vendor category used
  disproportionately by one group of employees would be a fairness problem
  we have not yet measured for (see Known Limitations in Eval Report —
  slice analysis covers vendor category and image quality, not employee
  demographics, because that data is not available to this system).

## Caveats and Recommendations
Do not deploy this checkpoint against a receipt distribution meaningfully
different from the training distribution (e.g., a non-US subsidiary)
without re-running the eval report against a matched sample first — the
85% US-vendor training skew means accuracy on an unmeasured distribution is
an unverified guess, not a number you can put in a design doc.
```

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The design doc explains the system we built."* No — a design doc that only explains is written backward. It should read as a decision made under uncertainty, with alternatives that could plausibly have won. If every alternative in your "Alternatives Considered" section is trivially bad, you didn't consider alternatives, you built strawmen to make the real decision look inevitable.
- *"A model card is the same information as the design doc, formatted differently."* No — the model card is scoped to the *artifact* (this specific checkpoint, its training data, its intended use), while the design doc is scoped to the *system* (the pipeline, the SLOs, the rollout). A model card should be reusable if you swap the serving stack; a design doc should not survive swapping the model.
- *"Non-goals are just goals we didn't get to yet."* No — a non-goal is a scope boundary you are *choosing to defend*, not a backlog item. "No handwritten receipts" isn't "handwriting support: not yet done," it's "if a handwritten receipt breaks this system, that is expected behavior, not a bug," and the doc exists partly to make that distinction unambiguous later.

### Lesson 2 — The Eval Report: turning a demo into a claim you can defend

**Teaching text**

An eval report is not "the accuracy number." It is the argument for why the accuracy number should be believed, which means it needs four things a bare number doesn't give you:

1. **Eval set provenance** — where the eval examples came from, who labeled them, and proof they weren't in the training data. Without this, "91.4% accuracy" could mean "91.4% accuracy on data the model memorized."
2. **Metric definitions**, written precisely enough that someone else could reimplement your scorer and get the same number. "Accuracy" is not a definition; "field is scored correct if the normalized string exact-matches the label, case-insensitive, currency-symbol-stripped" is.
3. **Slice analysis** — the aggregate number averaged over a population hides where it fails. A model that's 98% accurate on clean scans and 71% accurate on phone photos is not "91% accurate," it's two different systems wearing one number.
4. **Error analysis** — actual failure examples, not just a confusion matrix. A panel will ask "show me one it got wrong and tell me why," and "I don't know, it's a 91%" is a failed defense.

**Worked example — ReceiptIQ Eval Report (full)**

```markdown
# Eval Report — ReceiptIQ-extract-v1

​```claims
overall_field_accuracy_pct: 91.4
hallucination_rate_pct: 2.1
review_trigger_rate_pct: 18.3
eval_set_size: 500
​```

## Eval Set Provenance
500 receipts sampled from Finance Ops' archive, stratified by vendor
category (restaurant 32%, travel 24%, office supply 21%, other 23%) and by
image-quality tier (clean scan 41%, phone photo 47%, crumpled/faded 12%),
matching production traffic proportions measured over the prior quarter.
Held out from the AIE-301 LoRA fine-tune split at the data-split stage
(not filtered afterward) — confirmed by hash-checking eval receipt IDs
against the training manifest; zero overlap. Labeled by two annotators
independently; the 41 receipts (8.2%) where they disagreed on any field
were adjudicated by a third annotator. Inter-annotator agreement on the
other 459 was 97.6% field-level.

## Metric Definitions
- **Field accuracy (exact match):** predicted field string, after
  normalization (lowercase, strip currency symbols and thousands
  separators, parse dates to ISO-8601 before comparing), equals the
  adjudicated label string. Scored per field, then averaged.
- **Hallucination rate:** fraction of predicted fields where the predicted
  value has no supporting substring or reasonable inference (e.g. a
  computed tax from a stated subtotal+total counts as supported) anywhere
  in the source OCR text. Distinct from "wrong" — a hallucinated field is
  invented, not merely mis-transcribed.
- **Human-review trigger rate:** fraction of eval receipts where the
  confidence gate (any field confidence below 0.85, OR total_amount >
  $500) would route to human review rather than auto-post.

## Results — field accuracy by field
| Field | Accuracy | n |
|---|---|---|
| vendor | 94.2% | 500 |
| date | 96.8% | 500 |
| total_amount | 92.0% | 500 |
| currency | 99.1% | 500 (currency-labeled receipts only) |
| category | 88.2% | 500 |
| tax_amount | 85.4% | 471 (29 receipts have no separately stated tax) |
| **Overall (micro-avg across fields)** | **91.4%** | — |

Hallucination rate: 2.1% of predicted fields (most common: inventing a
`tax_amount` when the receipt states a tax-inclusive total with no
separate tax line — the model infers a plausible-looking split instead of
leaving the field null).

Human-review trigger rate: 18.3% — meaning 81.7% of eval-set receipts would
have auto-posted.

## Slice Analysis
**By image-quality tier**
| Tier | Overall field accuracy |
|---|---|
| Clean scan | 96.7% |
| Phone photo | 90.1% |
| Crumpled/faded | 74.8% |

The aggregate 91.4% is not representative of the crumpled/faded tier — that
tier is 12% of eval volume but accounts for 31% of all field errors. The
confidence gate catches most of this in practice (crumpled receipts produce
low-confidence predictions that route to review), but the raw accuracy gap
is real and should not be smoothed over by the aggregate number.

**By vendor category**
| Category | Category-field accuracy | Overall field accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Restaurant | 84.0% | 89.9% |
| Travel | 93.5% | 94.8% |
| Office supply | 91.2% | 93.1% |
| Other | 82.9% | 87.6% |

Restaurant and "other" categories underperform on the `category` field
specifically — restaurant receipts frequently span ambiguous categories
(client meal vs. team meal vs. personal), which is as much a label-quality
problem (annotators disagreed most on this category, 14 of the 41
adjudicated cases) as a model problem.

## Error Analysis — top failure modes
1. **Tax-inclusive totals → hallucinated tax split** (drives most of the
   2.1% hallucination rate). Example: OCR text `TOTAL $42.50 (incl. tax)`
   with no separate tax line; model predicts `tax_amount: 3.54` with no
   support. Fix candidate: constrain decoding to require tax_amount = null
   when no tax substring is present, rather than let the model infer.
2. **Crumpled-receipt vendor misreads** — OCR itself is noisy on this tier,
   and the model does not know to abstain; it extracts a plausible-looking
   vendor from partial/garbled text instead of flagging low confidence.
   Example: OCR text `STA*LES OFC` (torn receipt) → model predicts
   `Staples` with 0.91 confidence (should be much lower — the OCR text is
   genuinely ambiguous).
3. **Ambiguous meal category** — see restaurant slice above. Not purely a
   model failure; partially a label-definition gap the taxonomy needs to
   resolve (e.g., add "client meal" as its own category instead of forcing
   a binary).

## Known Limitations
- Eval set is US-vendor-skewed, matching training data (see Model Card).
  Accuracy on non-US vendor receipts is unmeasured, not "unlikely to be a
  problem" — those receipts are currently routed to review only because
  they're out of scope by design-doc non-goal, not because the eval
  proves they're handled safely.
- No fairness/demographic slice exists because employee demographic data
  is not joined to this eval set (see Model Card, Ethical Considerations).
  This is a known gap in the eval, not a claim that no such gap exists in
  the model.
```

**Named misconceptions**

- *"91.4% accuracy is the finding."* No — the finding is the whole slice table. 91.4% aggregate with a 74.8%/96.7% split hidden inside it is a materially different system than a flat 91.4%, and a defense panel will find the split in about one question if you don't show it first.
- *"Error analysis means a confusion matrix."* A confusion matrix tells you *that* errors cluster; it doesn't tell you *why*. The worked example's error analysis names a mechanism (tax-inclusive totals trigger hallucination) precise enough to generate a fix candidate. That's the bar — an error analysis that doesn't suggest a next action isn't finished.
- *"Provenance is a formality — everyone trusts the eval number."* The single most common way an eval report gets discredited in review is train/eval leakage. "How do you know these 500 weren't in the fine-tune?" needs a stronger answer than "we're pretty sure" — the worked example answers it with a specific verification method (hash-checked IDs against the training manifest), not a promise.

### Lesson 3 — The Cost Model: the number nobody wants to defend

**Teaching text**

Two costs get conflated constantly, and the conflation is where cost models lose credibility: **compute-only cost** (GPU-seconds, API tokens — the number that's easy to compute and flattering) and **fully-loaded cost** (compute + monitoring + storage + the amortized share of the on-call engineer's time who gets paged when it breaks). Fully-loaded cost is almost always dominated by *people*, not GPUs, for a system at moderate scale — and a cost model that reports only compute-only cost is not lying exactly, but it is answering a question nobody asked.

The other discipline a real cost model needs: **it has to stay honest even when the honest number embarrasses the decision you already made.** If the chosen architecture costs more than the rejected alternative, the cost model's job is to say so plainly and point back to the design doc's actual reason for the decision — not to quietly compute only the numbers that make the choice look cost-optimal after the fact.

Required sections: **Assumptions** (every input number, sourced), **Unit Economics** (cost per request, derived, not stated), **Sensitivity Analysis** (what happens if volume/prices move), **Breakeven vs. Alternatives** (the honest comparison).

**Worked example — the script that generates the numbers**

Real cost models are reproducible, not hand-typed into a table. This is the actual script behind ReceiptIQ's cost model:

```python
# cost_model.py — ReceiptIQ fully-loaded unit economics
# Python 3.12+, stdlib only.

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Assumptions:
    gpu_spot_usd_per_hr: float = 0.22          # L4, spot, us-region avg (AIE-303 pricing)
    business_hours_per_day: int = 9             # 7am-7pm minus low-traffic tail, rounded
    business_days_per_month: int = 22
    peak_headroom_gpus: int = 2                 # provisioned for peak concurrency, not sustained avg
    monthly_volume_requests: int = 1_200_000
    monitoring_usd_per_month: float = 40.0
    storage_usd_per_month: float = 15.0
    oncall_fte_fraction: float = 0.05           # amortized share of an engineer's time
    fully_loaded_engineer_usd_per_month: float = 12_000.0

    # Illustrative hosted-API baseline rates, used only for the
    # Breakeven vs. Alternatives comparison below — not a live price quote.
    api_input_usd_per_million_tokens: float = 0.15
    api_output_usd_per_million_tokens: float = 0.60
    avg_input_tokens_per_request: int = 380
    avg_output_tokens_per_request: int = 120
    api_oncall_fte_fraction: float = 0.02       # less ops burden without owning GPU serving


def compute_self_hosted(a: Assumptions) -> dict[str, float]:
    gpu_hours_per_month = a.peak_headroom_gpus * a.business_hours_per_day * a.business_days_per_month
    infra_usd_per_month = gpu_hours_per_month * a.gpu_spot_usd_per_hr
    oncall_usd_per_month = a.oncall_fte_fraction * a.fully_loaded_engineer_usd_per_month
    total_usd_per_month = (
        infra_usd_per_month
        + a.monitoring_usd_per_month
        + a.storage_usd_per_month
        + oncall_usd_per_month
    )
    return {
        "gpu_hours_per_month": gpu_hours_per_month,
        "infra_usd_per_month": round(infra_usd_per_month, 2),
        "oncall_usd_per_month": round(oncall_usd_per_month, 2),
        "total_usd_per_month": round(total_usd_per_month, 2),
        "compute_only_cost_per_request_usd": round(
            infra_usd_per_month / a.monthly_volume_requests, 7
        ),
        "fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd": round(
            total_usd_per_month / a.monthly_volume_requests, 7
        ),
    }


def compute_api_baseline(a: Assumptions) -> dict[str, float]:
    per_request_token_usd = (
        a.avg_input_tokens_per_request * a.api_input_usd_per_million_tokens
        + a.avg_output_tokens_per_request * a.api_output_usd_per_million_tokens
    ) / 1_000_000
    tokens_usd_per_month = per_request_token_usd * a.monthly_volume_requests
    oncall_usd_per_month = a.api_oncall_fte_fraction * a.fully_loaded_engineer_usd_per_month
    total_usd_per_month = (
        tokens_usd_per_month
        + a.monitoring_usd_per_month
        + a.storage_usd_per_month
        + oncall_usd_per_month
    )
    return {
        "tokens_usd_per_month": round(tokens_usd_per_month, 2),
        "total_usd_per_month": round(total_usd_per_month, 2),
        "fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd": round(
            total_usd_per_month / a.monthly_volume_requests, 7
        ),
    }


def sensitivity(a: Assumptions) -> list[dict[str, float]]:
    rows = []
    for volume_multiplier in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0):
        scaled = Assumptions(
            monthly_volume_requests=int(a.monthly_volume_requests * volume_multiplier)
        )
        result = compute_self_hosted(scaled)
        rows.append(
            {
                "volume_multiplier": volume_multiplier,
                "monthly_volume_requests": scaled.monthly_volume_requests,
                "fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd": result["fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd"],
            }
        )
    return rows


if __name__ == "__main__":
    a = Assumptions()
    print("-- self-hosted --")
    for k, v in compute_self_hosted(a).items():
        print(f"{k}: {v}")
    print("-- api baseline --")
    for k, v in compute_api_baseline(a).items():
        print(f"{k}: {v}")
    print("-- sensitivity (self-hosted, volume) --")
    for row in sensitivity(a):
        print(row)
```

## Assumptions
All inputs and their source are enumerated in `cost_model.py::Assumptions`.
Highlights: GPU spot price $0.22/hr (L4, us-region average measured in
AIE-303); provisioned for 2x peak-headroom GPUs during 9 business

## Unit Economics
Computed by `cost_model.py`, not hand-entered:
- Compute-only cost per request: **$0.0000726**
- Fully-loaded cost per request (compute + monitoring + storage +
  amortized on-call): **$0.0006184**

The fully-loaded number is **8.5x** the compute-only number. On-call
($600/month) alone is larger than infra ($87.12/month) — at this volume,
the dominant cost is people-time, not GPU-time. Reporting only the
compute-only number would understate true cost by an order of magnitude.

Fully-loaded cost per request ($0.0006184) is within the design doc's
budget of $0.003/request.

## Sensitivity Analysis
| Volume | Fully-loaded $/request |
|---|---|
| 0.5x (600K/mo) | $0.0012369 |
| 1.0x (1.2M/mo, current) | $0.0006184 |
| 2.0x (2.4M/mo) | $0.0003092 |
| 4.0x (4.8M/mo) | $0.0001546 |

Cost per request roughly halves as volume doubles, because on-call and
monitoring are fixed costs that amortize — infra cost is the only line
that scales with volume, and it's the smaller line. This means the current
architecture gets *more* cost-efficient at higher volume, not less; the
budget headroom in the design doc ($0.003 target vs. $0.0006 actual) is
not at risk from growth.

## Breakeven vs. Alternatives
**Honest finding: self-hosted is more expensive, fully loaded, than the
hosted-API alternative would have been** — $0.0006184/request self-hosted
vs. $0.0003748/request estimated for the GPT-4o-mini-API alternative
(illustrative rates; see Assumptions), a difference of about $292/month at
current volume. The design doc did not choose self-hosting because it was
cheaper. It chose self-hosting because the API alternative fails threat
model finding **T2** (PII in receipt text leaving the infrastructure
boundary to a third party) at a volume and compliance posture Legal did
not clear. This cost model is not the argument for the architecture — the
threat model is. This section exists so that argument isn't hidden behind

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The cost model exists to justify the decision already made."* This is the single most damaging misconception in this lesson, and the worked example is built specifically to demonstrate the fix: the honest cost comparison here makes the chosen architecture look *worse* on cost, and the document says so directly instead of stopping the analysis at a flattering number.
- *"GPU cost is the cost."* At the scale of most capstone systems, on-call and monitoring dominate. A cost model that models only compute is modeling the cheap part.
- *"Sensitivity analysis means 'what if we 10x traffic and everything breaks.'"* It should also cover the boring direction — what happens to unit economics if volume *drops*, since fixed costs (monitoring, on-call) don't shrink with volume and can make a system look worse per-unit exactly when it's least used.

### Lesson 4 — The Threat Model and the Incident Runbook: the pair that has to agree with each other

**Teaching text**

A threat model without a runbook is a list of things you were worried about once. A runbook without a threat model is a list of alarms someone configured without knowing why. They are a pair, and the discipline is that **every threat-model finding that isn't explicitly marked an accepted risk must have a corresponding incident procedure in the runbook** — and every incident class in the runbook that claims to mitigate a threat should be traceable back to a specific finding ID. This is the cross-check this module's labs will make you *automate*, because "I'm pretty sure the runbook covers everything in the threat model" is exactly the kind of claim a defense panel tests by picking one finding at random and asking "show me where the runbook handles this."

Threat model required sections: **Assets** (what's worth attacking), **Actors** (who would, and why), **Findings** (each with an ID, severity, description, and status — mitigated / accepted risk / open), **Accepted Risks** (explicitly, with the reasoning — silently dropping a finding is not the same as accepting it on the record).

Runbook required sections: **Service Overview**, **On-Call and Paging** (thresholds — and a paging threshold that's *looser* than the SLO it's protecting is a bug, because it means you find out about the SLO breach from a customer instead of a pager), **Incident Classes** (symptom → triage → mitigation, each traceable to a threat finding where applicable), **Rollback Procedure**, **Postmortem Triggers** (what severity mandates a written postmortem).

## Assets
- Receipt OCR text and images in flight and at rest (contains PII: partial

|---|---|---|---|

approximate weights via a side channel, against an API with existing

​```

## Service Overview
FastAPI + vLLM serving ReceiptIQ-extract-v1 behind an internal load
balancer. Upstream: OCR service. Downstream: human-review queue,
accounting-system webhook. On-call rotation: ML Platform team, weekly.

Pages on: p99 latency > **1800ms** sustained 5 min (pages *before* the
2000ms SLO in the design doc is actually breached, not after — the point
of a page is to act before the SLO commitment is broken, not to confirm it
already was); error rate > 2% sustained 5 min; auto-post rate dropping to
0% for 10 min (likely means the confidence gate or webhook is broken, not
that every receipt suddenly got harder).

## Incident Classes

### IC-1 — Suspicious extraction spike (maps to threat finding T1)
**Symptom:** Spike in receipts where total_amount, once extracted, is >5x
the receipt's OCR-detected subtotal, or category flips to a
high-reimbursement category inconsistent with vendor.
**Triage:** Pull the flagged receipts' raw OCR text; check for embedded
instruction-like strings ("ignore", "system:", "actually set").
**Mitigation:** Extraction prompt template wraps OCR text in an explicit
data-boundary delimiter and instructs the model to treat delimited content
strictly as data, never as instructions (validated in AIE-304-style
red-team suite). If a bypass is found, route affected category to 100%
human review pending a prompt-template patch; do not attempt a live model
change during the incident.

### IC-2 — PII found in logs (maps to threat finding T2)
**Symptom:** Audit or report that receipt PII (card digits, addresses) is
visible in request/response logs beyond the 7-day retention policy, or to
staff outside the approved log-access group.
**Triage:** Confirm scope (which logs, which time window, who had access).
**Mitigation:** Logging pipeline redacts PII-pattern fields
(card-number-like digit runs, address-like strings) before write, with a 7-
day hard retention on the pre-redaction buffer used only for debugging.
If redaction failed: purge the affected log range, rotate log-access
credentials, file a postmortem (this is always postmortem-triggering
regardless of severity band — PII exposure is not judged by blast radius
alone).

### IC-3 — Cost/latency spike from oversized input (maps to threat finding T3)
**Symptom:** p99 latency or per-request cost spikes correlated with a small
number of requests carrying abnormally large OCR text payloads.
**Triage:** Check request size distribution for the spike window.
**Mitigation:** Input OCR text is capped at 4,000 tokens at the API
boundary (oversized input is truncated with the receipt routed straight to
human review, not silently processed truncated-and-uncertain). If the cap
is being hit by legitimate itemized receipts rather than adversarial
input, that's a design-doc non-goal boundary being tested in practice —
escalate to product, don't just raise the cap.

### IC-4 — Latency SLO risk (general)
**Symptom:** Paging threshold above (1800ms sustained) fires.
**Triage:** Check GPU utilization/batch depth (saturation) vs. upstream OCR
service latency (dependency) vs. a bad deploy (regression).
**Mitigation:** If saturation — provisioned headroom (2 GPUs) should absorb
normal peaks; sustained saturation past headroom pages for manual scale-up.
If regression — roll back (see Rollback Procedure).

### IC-5 — Feedback-loop drift (maps to threat finding T5)
**Symptom:** Quarterly retraining shows category accuracy *regressing* on
categories where auto-accept rate in the review queue is unusually high
(reviewers agreeing with the model at a rate above their historical
baseline, suggesting rubber-stamping rather than genuine correction).
**Triage:** Sample a batch of "reviewer agreed" corrections for the
suspect category, re-verify by an auditor blind to the model's output.
**Mitigation:** This finding is marked "mitigated (partial)" in the threat
model because the only current defense is the quarterly audit sample
above — there is no automated defense yet. If drift is confirmed,
exclude the suspect period's review-queue data from the next retrain and
escalate to product for a reviewer-workflow fix (e.g. blind review, where
the reviewer doesn't see the model's draft until after entering their own
read).

## Rollback Procedure
Prior model version and prior prompt-template version are both pinned and
deployable via the same release pipeline (single command, <5 min).
Rollback does not require a new fine-tune or eval cycle — it re-activates
an already-evaluated prior artifact.

## Postmortem Triggers
Any IC-2 (PII), any incident causing incorrect auto-post at >$500 (bypasses
the confidence gate's own threshold, meaning the gate itself failed), or
any incident with >30 min of degraded auto-post availability during
business hours.
```

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The runbook should list every threat-model finding to look thorough."* No — T4 is deliberately *not* in the runbook, and that's correct, because it's an accepted risk with a documented reason. Padding the runbook with a procedure for an accepted risk doesn't make the pair more consistent, it makes the "accepted risk" designation meaningless.
- *"Paging thresholds should match the SLO exactly."* A paging threshold equal to (or looser than) the SLO means you're notified exactly when, or after, you've already broken your promise. IC-4's threshold (1800ms) is deliberately tighter than the SLO (2000ms) — the gap is the time to act before the commitment breaks.
- *"A finding is 'mitigated' once there's a runbook entry for it."* IC-5 has a runbook entry and is still marked "mitigated (partial)" in the threat model, honestly, because the entry is an audit-and-escalate procedure, not a prevention. Writing a triage step is not the same as closing the finding, and the two documents need to agree on that distinction, not just on the finding's existence.

        "Incident Classes",
        "Rollback Procedure",
        "Postmortem Triggers",
    ],
}


def extract_headers(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Return the list of ## header titles, in document order."""
    return [m.group(1) for m in HEADER_RE.finditer(text)]


def check_required_sections(text: str, doc_type: str) -> list[str]:
    """Return the list of required section names MISSING from text.
    Empty list means the doc passes the completeness gate.

    A required section counts as present if it appears as a whole-word
    match inside an actual header, not only on an exact full-string match.
    Real documents routinely write a descriptive header — e.g. this
    module's own ReceiptIQ Eval Report uses
    "## Results — field accuracy by field" and
    "## Error Analysis — top failure modes" rather than the bare
    "## Results" / "## Error Analysis" — and the required section is still
    unambiguously present there. An exact-match check would wrongly flag
    both as missing (as this checker once did, against the module's own
    worked example). Word-boundary matching still refuses a false hit like
    a header titled "## ResultsTable", which does not contain "Results" as
    a whole word.
    """
    headers = extract_headers(text)
    required = REQUIRED_SECTIONS[doc_type]
    missing = []
    for section in required:
        pattern = re.compile(r"\b" + re.escape(section) + r"\b")
        if not any(pattern.search(header) for header in headers):
            missing.append(section)
    return missing

```python
"""Parse machine-checkable ```claims blocks out of artifact markdown, and
cross-check claims between artifacts."""
import operator
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable

CLAIMS_BLOCK_RE = re.compile(r"```claims\n(.*?)```", re.DOTALL)

OPS: dict[str, Callable[[object, object], bool]] = {
    "<=": operator.le,
    ">=": operator.ge,
    "==": operator.eq,
}

# Fields the claims-block schema declares as list-typed. Whether a value
# becomes a list must be decided from THIS declared type, not from whether
# the raw string happens to contain a comma — a list with exactly one
# element (e.g. `accepted_risk_ids: T4`) has no comma and would otherwise
# be misclassified as a scalar.
LIST_VALUED_KEYS: set[str] = {"finding_ids", "accepted_risk_ids", "handled_finding_ids"}


def _coerce(raw: str) -> object:
    try:
        return int(raw)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    try:
        return float(raw)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    return raw


def parse_claims(text: str) -> dict[str, object]:
    match = CLAIMS_BLOCK_RE.search(text)
    if not match:
        return {}
    claims: dict[str, object] = {}
    for line in match.group(1).strip().splitlines():
        line = line.strip()
        if not line or ":" not in line:
            continue
        key, _, raw_value = line.partition(":")
        key = key.strip()
        raw_value = raw_value.strip()
        if key in LIST_VALUED_KEYS:
            # Declared list-typed: always a list, even a single bare token
            # with no comma (a one-element list, not a scalar).
            claims[key] = [v.strip() for v in raw_value.split(",")]
        else:
            claims[key] = _coerce(raw_value)
    return claims


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class CrossCheck:
    description: str
    check: Callable[[dict[str, dict]], "str | None"]


def compare(doc_a: str, key_a: str, op: str, doc_b: str, key_b: str) -> Callable:
    def _check(claims_by_doc: dict[str, dict]) -> "str | None":
        a = claims_by_doc[doc_a][key_a]
        b = claims_by_doc[doc_b][key_b]
        if not OPS[op](a, b):
            return f"{doc_a}.{key_a}={a!r} fails '{op}' against {doc_b}.{key_b}={b!r}"
        return None
    return _check


def subset_minus_accepted(
    finding_doc: str, findings_key: str, accepted_key: str,
    handling_doc: str, handled_key: str,
) -> Callable:
    def _check(claims_by_doc: dict[str, dict]) -> "str | None":
        findings = set(claims_by_doc[finding_doc][findings_key])
        accepted = set(claims_by_doc[finding_doc].get(accepted_key, []))
        handled = set(claims_by_doc[handling_doc][handled_key])
        required = findings - accepted
        missing = required - handled
        if missing:
            return f"{handling_doc}.{handled_key} is missing required findings: {sorted(missing)}"
        return None
    return _check


def run_cross_checks(
    claims_by_doc: dict[str, dict], checks: list[CrossCheck]
) -> list[str]:
    violations = []
    for c in checks:
        result = c.check(claims_by_doc)
        if result is not None:
            violations.append(f"[{c.description}] {result}")
    return violations


CROSS_CHECKS = [
    CrossCheck(
        "runbook pages before the latency SLO is actually breached",
        compare("runbook", "latency_page_threshold_ms", "<=", "design_doc", "p99_latency_slo_ms"),
    ),
    CrossCheck(
        "fully-loaded cost stays under the design doc's budget",
        compare("cost_model", "fully_loaded_cost_per_request_usd", "<=", "design_doc", "cost_per_request_budget_usd"),
    ),
    CrossCheck(
        "measured eval accuracy meets the design doc's target",
        compare("eval_report", "overall_field_accuracy_pct", ">=", "design_doc", "field_accuracy_target_pct"),
    ),
    CrossCheck(
        "runbook handles every non-accepted threat-model finding",
        subset_minus_accepted("threat_model", "finding_ids", "accepted_risk_ids", "runbook", "handled_finding_ids"),
    ),
]

Solution — `run_gate.py`:

```python
"""Combined completeness + cross-consistency gate for a six-artifact bundle."""
import sys
from pathlib import Path

from artifact_gate.completeness import check_required_sections
from artifact_gate.claims import parse_claims, run_cross_checks, CROSS_CHECKS

DOC_FILES = {
    "design_doc": "design_doc.md",
    "model_card": "model_card.md",
    "eval_report": "eval_report.md",
    "cost_model": "cost_model.md",
    "threat_model": "threat_model.md",
    "runbook": "runbook.md",
}


def load_bundle(directory: Path) -> dict[str, str]:
    bundle = {}
    for doc_type, filename in DOC_FILES.items():
        path = directory / filename
        if not path.exists():
            raise FileNotFoundError(
                f"Bundle is missing required artifact: {filename} "
                f"(expected at {path})"
            )
        bundle[doc_type] = path.read_text()
    return bundle


def run_gate(directory: Path) -> int:
    bundle = load_bundle(directory)

    completeness_failures = {}
    for doc_type, text in bundle.items():
        missing = check_required_sections(text, doc_type)
        if missing:
            completeness_failures[doc_type] = missing

    claims_by_doc = {doc_type: parse_claims(text) for doc_type, text in bundle.items()}
    cross_violations = run_cross_checks(claims_by_doc, CROSS_CHECKS)

    if not completeness_failures and not cross_violations:
        print("GATE: PASS — all six artifacts complete and cross-consistent.")
        return 0

    print("GATE: FAIL")
    if completeness_failures:
        print("\nMissing required sections:")
        for doc_type, missing in completeness_failures.items():
            print(f"  {doc_type}: {missing}")
    if cross_violations:
        print("\nCross-artifact consistency violations:")
        for v in cross_violations:
            print(f"  {v}")
    return 1


if __name__ == "__main__":
    directory = Path(sys.argv[1])
    sys.exit(run_gate(directory))
```

## Assessment

Gaming-resistant by construction: the automated gates (Lab 1, Lab 2) can be passed by a bundle with real content or a bundle with empty-but-structurally-valid sections — so the rubric weights the judgment dimensions the gate cannot see at least as heavily as the two it can.

| Dimension | Fail | Pass | Distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Section completeness** (automated, Lab 1 gate) | Gate reports missing sections on final submission | Gate passes on all six artifacts | Gate passed on first run against a bundle where every section is substantively filled, not placeholder text |
| **Cross-artifact consistency** (automated, Lab 2/3 gate) | Gate reports unresolved violations on final submission | Gate passes | Gate passed *and* the learner's writeup names a violation the gate caught during drafting and what it revealed about a real inconsistency (not a hypothetical one) |
| **Alternatives Considered depth** (design doc, human-graded) | Zero or trivially-bad rejected alternatives ("do nothing" as the only option) | ≥2 genuinely plausible rejected alternatives, each with a specific reason tied to evidence (a number, a threat finding, a prototype result) | A rejected alternative is later referenced elsewhere in the artifact set (e.g. the cost model's Breakeven section), showing the alternatives analysis is load-bearing, not decorative |
| **Cost model honesty** | Reports only compute-only cost, or only the number that flatters the chosen architecture | Reports fully-loaded cost and at least one honest unfavorable comparison if one exists | The unfavorable finding (if any) is explicitly reconciled with the design doc's real reason for the decision, in the cost model's own text, the way the ReceiptIQ exemplar does |
| **Threat model → runbook coverage judgment** | Runbook is missing a handler for a non-accepted finding, or "handles" a finding with a procedure that doesn't match its severity | Every non-accepted finding has a runbook entry (gate-checked); accepted risks are justified with a specific reason, not "low priority" | Runbook entries distinguish prevention from detection/audit-only mitigation honestly (as IC-5's "mitigated (partial)" does), rather than all reading as fully solved |
| **Eval report error analysis** | Aggregate metric only, or a confusion matrix with no named failure mechanism | At least 2 real failure examples with a named mechanism per failure mode, plus a slice analysis that would change a reader's trust in the aggregate number | A named failure mode is traceable to a specific, actionable fix candidate that could be implemented (not "collect more data") |
| **Prediction rigor** (across all three labs' Predict steps) | Predictions are vague, unfalsifiable, or written after the Build step ("post-dated") | Each prediction states a specific claim and the mechanism behind it, made before the corresponding Build/run | Predictions are checked against actual results in the writeup, including at least one prediction that was *wrong*, with a stated reason why |
| **Defense readiness** (the ethics-thread question, applied) | "Who bears the cost if you're wrong" answered with a category ("users," "the business") or left unanswered | Answered with a specific real person, role, or group in the learner's own system, with a concrete failure scenario | The answer is cross-checked against the runbook's Postmortem Triggers — the named failure scenario is either covered by a trigger, or the gap is stated explicitly as a known limitation, not silently left out |

A submission that passes both automated gates but scores Fail on four or more of the human-graded rows has produced a *structurally valid, substantively hollow* artifact set — which is precisely the failure mode this rubric is built to catch, and precisely why the gates alone are not the assessment.

## Elle pacing notes

**The wall, and it's not where you'd expect.** Learners who breezed through AIE-401-M01–M03's code hit a wall here that has nothing to do with code: writing "here is a rejected alternative and why it lost" is a genuinely different cognitive task than writing code that runs, and learners who are strong engineers often produce a design doc with a perfect System Overview and a one-sentence, hand-waved Alternatives Considered ("we also thought about doing it differently but decided this way was better"). That's not laziness — it's that engineers are trained to defend the chosen path, not the rejected one, and defending a rejected path convincingly is an unfamiliar rhetorical move. Name this explicitly before the learner hits it, not after: "you are about to feel stuck on a section that has no code in it — that's expected, and it's not a sign you're behind."

**The reroute for the Alternatives wall.** Don't tell the learner to "just write two more sentences." Ask them: "what's the second-best tool/approach a colleague with different priors would have picked, and specifically why did it lose — cost, latency, compliance, data, or timeline?" Naming the *axis* the alternative lost on turns a vague hand-wave into a real comparison almost immediately. If they can't name an axis, that's diagnostic — it usually means they haven't actually considered an alternative, and the reroute is to make them spend 20 minutes prototyping (or seriously estimating) the alternative before writing about it, not writing around the gap.

**The number-chasing rabbit hole (cost model).** The opposite failure mode from the Alternatives wall: some learners, especially ones who enjoyed AIE-303's cost-accounting work, will disappear into refining the cost model's precision — third-decimal-place GPU pricing, elaborate multi-scenario sensitivity grids — while the design doc and threat model sit half-finished. This is *productive* struggle for about 30 minutes (getting the unit economics right matters) and *blocked* struggle past that (a cost model accurate to 4 significant figures attached to a design doc with no SLOs to check it against is wasted precision). The accelerate condition: once `cost_model.py` runs and produces a number that's in the right order of magnitude and is reproducible from stated assumptions, stop tuning it and move to the next artifact — precision tuning can happen in a final pass once all six exist and the cross-checker can actually use the number for something.

**The cross-checker debugging loop is where real learning happens — let it be slow.** When Lab 2's or Lab 3's gate fails against a learner's own bundle, the instinct is to fix the *number* that's flagged as fast as possible to turn the gate green. Slow this down deliberately: before editing anything, have the learner state which document has the wrong number and why — is the design doc's SLO stale because the system got faster after the doc was written, or is the runbook's threshold wrong because it was copy-pasted from a template? Those have different fixes (update the doc vs. update the number), and a learner who just edits until green without answering that question will produce a bundle that's internally consistent and still wrong about the system. This is the highest-value 20 minutes in the module; don't let it get rushed to hit a green checkmark.

**Defense-readiness anxiety, late in the module.** By Lab 3's third Predict item (naming who bears the cost, concretely), some learners — especially ones building a capstone with real stakeholders (a tool for their actual employer, a system touching real users) — will get genuinely uncomfortable, because naming a specific person who's harmed by their own system's failure mode is not an abstract exercise for them. This discomfort is the point of Ethics Tier 2/3 for this course, not a sign the exercise is miscalibrated — but watch for the difference between *productive* discomfort (the learner names someone real and sits with it) and *avoidant* answers (reverting to "users" after being asked to be specific, or picking a deliberately low-stakes hypothetical to avoid the real one). If you see the avoidant pattern, the reroute is not to push harder on the same question — it's to ask a smaller, more concrete version first ("who did you personally talk to while building this") and build up from there.

**Accelerate condition.** A learner whose Lab 1 and Lab 2 gates pass clean on the *first* attempt against their own bundle (not the exemplar's) is very likely someone who wrote the claims blocks to match the prose after the fact, or who under-scoped their threat model to make the subset check trivial (few findings, most marked accepted-risk). Don't accelerate this learner to Lab 3 on the green checkmark alone — spot-check by asking them to explain one specific cross-check violation the exemplar's Lab 2 draft had, in their own words, before moving on. If they can, they understood the mechanism and a clean first pass is genuine skill. If they can't, send them back to seed one deliberate inconsistency into their own bundle and watch the checker catch it, so the tool's value is felt, not just observed in the exemplar.

**Tier 2 — What the field's own artifact-set practice suppresses.**
Published model cards from major labs and published capstone/case-study writeups share a pattern: the Ethical Considerations section is real but the Caveats and Recommendations section is frequently a formality, and the cost model — when one is published at all — almost never shows the unfavorable comparison the way this module's exemplar does. The skill this module teaches — write the artifact set to be true, not to be flattering — is explicitly a skill that most public examples of "artifact sets" will not model for you, because the incentive that produced them was different from the incentive this course is asking you to operate under.

**Fix applied (the solution in Lab 1):** `check_required_sections` now matches a required section name as a whole-word match inside the actual header text, rather than requiring the header to equal the required name exactly:

```python
def check_required_sections(text: str, doc_type: str) -> list[str]:
    headers = extract_headers(text)
    required = REQUIRED_SECTIONS[doc_type]
    missing = []
    for section in required:
        pattern = re.compile(r"\b" + re.escape(section) + r"\b")
        if not any(pattern.search(header) for header in headers):
            missing.append(section)
    return missing
```

This is also more realistic for how real documents actually write section headers (a subtitle after an em-dash is normal practice, not a formatting error).
